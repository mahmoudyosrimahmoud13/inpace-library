# Read: Markov chain Monte Carlo and Langevin sampling (14.2–14.3)

Read sections 14.2 and 14.3 to the end of the chapter (pages 440 to 458).

## Markov chain Monte Carlo (14.2)

Build a chain of samples where each one is a small random change of the last, designed so that, after a while, the
samples follow p.

- **The Metropolis algorithm** (14.2.1): propose z* = z + small random step (a symmetric proposal). Accept with
  probability `min(1, p̃(z*) / p̃(z))`; otherwise stay at z (and record z again). Only **ratios** of p̃ are needed, so the
  unknown normalizing constant cancels. That's the magic.
- **Markov chains** (14.2.2): read why it works. A chain has p as its stationary distribution if it satisfies **detailed
  balance**: the flow from z to z′ equals the flow back. It must also be able to reach every state (ergodic). Successive
  samples are **correlated**, and the first ones (the **burn-in**) still remember the starting point, so discard them.
- **Metropolis–Hastings** (14.2.3): the general version, for proposals that aren't symmetric; the acceptance ratio gains
  a correction factor.
- **Gibbs sampling** (14.2.4): update one variable at a time, sampling it from its conditional given all the others (in a
  graphical model, only its Markov blanket matters). Every proposal is accepted.
- **Ancestral sampling** (14.2.5): for directed graphs with nothing observed, no chain is needed; you did this on day 1.

## Langevin sampling (14.3)

The bridge to modern generative models. Read it carefully.

- **Energy-based models** (14.3.1): write any density as `p(x) = exp(−E(x)) / Z`. A network can output the energy E(x),
  but the normalizer Z is an impossible integral.
- **Maximizing the likelihood** (14.3.2) still works, if you can draw samples from the current model to estimate one
  term of the gradient.
- **Langevin dynamics** (14.3.3): move a sample **downhill in energy (uphill in log-probability) plus noise**:

```text
x ← x + η ∇ₓ log p(x) + √(2η) · ε,      ε ~ N(0, I)
```

It only needs the **score** `∇ₓ log p(x)`, the gradient of the log-density with respect to the data, and the score
doesn't depend on Z. Remember this formula: in part 5, a diffusion model is a network that learns the score, and
generating an image is (almost exactly) running Langevin dynamics.
