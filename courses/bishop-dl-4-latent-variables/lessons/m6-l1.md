# Read: principal component analysis (16.1)

Read the opening of chapter 16 and section 16.1 (pages 495 to 506).

## Continuous latent variables

Chapter 15's latent variable was discrete (which cluster). Here it's **continuous**: the data lies near a low-dimensional
surface, and the latent variable is the position on it. The opening example makes it concrete: images of one digit,
shifted and rotated, form a 3-dimensional family inside a space of thousands of pixel values.

## Two ways to the same answer (16.1.1–16.1.2)

PCA finds an M-dimensional linear subspace for D-dimensional data. The book derives it twice, and both give the same
result:

- **Maximum variance**: choose the directions along which the projected data spreads out the most.
- **Minimum error**: choose the subspace that makes the squared distance from each point to its projection smallest.

Either way, the answer is the **M eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalues**. Each
eigenvalue is the variance along its direction, and the error of keeping M components is the sum of the eigenvalues you
dropped.

## Applications (16.1.3–16.1.5)

- **Compression** (16.1.3): store M numbers per data point instead of D. Look at the digit reconstructions with
  increasing M (Figure 16.5): the first few components already capture the overall shape.
- **Whitening** (16.1.4): rotate onto the eigenvectors and rescale each by its standard deviation, so the data has zero
  mean and identity covariance. A useful pre-processing step (part 2's normalization, done properly).
- **High-dimensional data** (16.1.5): with fewer points N than dimensions D, at most N − 1 eigenvalues are non-zero, and
  there's a trick that works with an N × N matrix instead of D × D. Read it; it matters for images.
