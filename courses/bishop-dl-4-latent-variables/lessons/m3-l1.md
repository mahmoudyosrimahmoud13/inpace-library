# Read: basic sampling algorithms (14.1)

Read the opening of chapter 14 and section 14.1 (pages 429 to 440).

## Why sample?

Most questions about a probability model are **expectations**: averages over the distribution (14.1.1). With L
independent samples, `E[f] ≈ (1/L) Σ f(z⁽ˡ⁾)`, and the error shrinks like 1/√L **whatever the dimension**. That's why
sampling works where grids and sums fail. The hard part is getting the samples.

## The basic tools

- **Standard distributions** (14.1.2): a uniform random number pushed through the **inverse cumulative distribution
  function** has the distribution you want. It's the change-of-variables formula from section 2.4, used backwards. The
  Box–Muller method turns two uniforms into two Gaussians.
- **Rejection sampling** (14.1.3): if you can evaluate `p̃(z)` (p up to a constant) and you have a simple q with
  `k·q(z) ≥ p̃(z)` everywhere, draw z from q, and keep it with probability `p̃(z) / (k q(z))`. The kept samples follow p
  exactly. The catch, which the book quantifies: the acceptance rate falls **exponentially** with dimension. Even a 1%
  mismatch in width gives an acceptance rate of about 1 in 20,000 in 1,000 dimensions.
- **Adaptive rejection sampling** (14.1.4): build the envelope on the fly for log-concave densities. Skim.
- **Importance sampling** (14.1.5): don't throw samples away; **weight** them by `p(z)/q(z)`. It estimates expectations
  directly, without producing samples of p. It fails silently when q misses regions where p is large: a few huge weights
  dominate.
- **Sampling-importance-resampling** (14.1.6): draw from q, weight, then resample according to the weights to get
  approximate samples of p.

## The lesson of this section

All of these depend on a simple proposal q that is close to p. In high dimensions that's almost impossible, which is
exactly why the next section turns to Markov chains: instead of proposing from scratch, take small steps from the
current sample.
