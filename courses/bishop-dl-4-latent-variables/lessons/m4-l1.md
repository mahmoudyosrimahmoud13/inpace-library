# Read: K-means and mixtures of Gaussians (15.1–15.2)

Read the opening of chapter 15 and sections 15.1 and 15.2 (pages 459 to 474).

## Latent variables

The chapter's idea: explain the data with **hidden** (latent) variables. Each data point came from one of K groups, but
the labels were never written down. Introducing a latent variable for "which group" makes complicated distributions
simple, and gives algorithms that are easy to derive.

## K-means (15.1)

The simplest version, with no probabilities at all. Find K centres μₖ, and an assignment of each point to one centre, so
as to minimize the **distortion** J: the sum of squared distances from each point to its centre. Alternate two steps:

1. **Assign** each point to its nearest centre (the centres stay fixed).
2. **Update** each centre to the mean of its points (the assignments stay fixed).

Each step can only lower J, so the algorithm always converges, though perhaps to a local minimum: run it from several
starting points. Read the image example (15.1.1): treat every pixel's colour as a data point, cluster into K colours, and
repaint each pixel with its centre's colour. That's **lossy compression** (vector quantization): you store only K colours
plus one small index per pixel. You'll build it today.

## Mixtures of Gaussians (15.2)

The probabilistic version. A latent one-hot variable z picks a component with probability πₖ, then x is drawn from that
component's Gaussian:

```text
p(x) = Σₖ πₖ N(x | μₖ, Σₖ)
```

Two quantities to understand well:

- The **responsibility** γ(zₙₖ) (from Bayes' theorem): the posterior probability that point n came from component k. It's a
  **soft** assignment, where K-means made a hard one.
- The **likelihood** (15.2.1–15.2.2): the log of a sum, which has no closed-form maximum. Setting its derivatives to zero
  gives equations in which the responsibilities depend on the parameters and vice versa. That suggests an iterative
  algorithm: tomorrow's EM.

Read the warning about **singularities**: if a component collapses onto a single data point, its variance goes to zero and
the likelihood to infinity. Maximum likelihood over-fits again; in practice, detect it and reset the component, or add a
small floor to the variances.
