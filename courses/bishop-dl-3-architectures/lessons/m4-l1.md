# Read: natural language and transformer language models (12.2–12.3)

Read sections 12.2 and 12.3 (pages 374 to 394).

## From text to vectors (12.2)

- **Word embeddings** (12.2.1): turn each word into a learned vector, by training a simple network to predict a word
  from its neighbours, or the neighbours from the word. Words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors, and
  some relationships even become directions in the embedding space.
- **Tokenization** (12.2.2): real models don't use whole words or single characters, but pieces in between. **Byte pair
  encoding** starts from characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair. Common words become one token;
  rare words split into several.
- **Bag of words** (12.2.3): ignore order completely. Surprisingly useful for some tasks, useless for others.
- **Autoregressive models** (12.2.4): the product rule from chapter 2, applied to a sequence:
  `p(x₁, …, x_N) = Π p(xₙ | x₁, …, xₙ₋₁)`. Predicting the next token, again and again, is a complete model of text.
- **Recurrent networks and backpropagation through time** (12.2.5–12.2.6): the older way to model sequences: process one
  token at a time and carry a hidden state. Read to understand why transformers replaced them: RNNs can't be computed in
  parallel across the sequence, and gradients struggle to travel back through many steps.

## Transformer language models (12.3)

- **Decoder transformers** (12.3.1): the GPT family. A stack of transformer layers with **masked (causal) attention**, so
  each position only sees earlier tokens, and a linear-softmax output that predicts the next token. Thanks to the mask,
  one forward pass trains on **every** position of a sequence at once. Study Figure 12.15.
- **Sampling strategies** (12.3.2): how to turn next-token probabilities into text. **Greedy** search (always the most
  probable) and **beam search** give dull, repetitive text. **Top-K** and **nucleus (top-p)** sampling draw from the most
  likely tokens. **Temperature** T reshapes the softmax: T < 1 is safer, T > 1 more surprising.
- **Encoder transformers** (12.3.3): BERT. No mask, so every token sees the whole sentence. Trained by hiding random
  tokens and predicting them, then fine-tuned for tasks like classification.
- **Sequence-to-sequence** (12.3.4): an encoder reads the input, a decoder writes the output, connected by
  **cross-attention**. The original transformer, built for translation.
- **Large language models** (12.3.5): the same decoder, scaled up enormously in data, parameters and compute, then
  fine-tuned to follow instructions. Read the book's notes on what scale brings.
