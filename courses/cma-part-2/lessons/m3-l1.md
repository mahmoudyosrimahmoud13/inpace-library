# Section A: earnings quality and analytical issues

Ratios are only as good as the numbers behind them. Section A also asks you to spot when reported numbers **mislead**.

## Earnings quality

High-quality earnings are **sustainable, backed by cash, and based on conservative choices**. Warning signs:

| Sign | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Net income rising, operating cash flow flat or falling | Profit may come from accruals, not cash |
| Receivables growing faster than sales | Aggressive revenue recognition, or weak collections |
| Inventory growing faster than sales | Obsolete stock, or overproduction to absorb fixed overhead |
| Big "one-time" items every year | Recurring costs labeled as non-recurring |
| Frequent changes in estimates (useful lives, bad debt %) | Managing earnings |
| Capitalizing costs others expense | Inflates assets and profit |

**Accrual ratio:** (Net income − Operating cash flow) ÷ Average total assets. Higher means lower quality.

## Earnings management techniques the exam names

- **Cookie jar reserves:** over-provisioning in good years, releasing in bad years.
- **Big bath:** taking huge write-offs in a bad year so future years look better.
- **Channel stuffing:** shipping excess goods to distributors at period end.
- **Bill-and-hold:** recording sales for goods not yet delivered.
- **Changing depreciation lives** or **capitalizing** operating costs.

## Off-balance-sheet items

Obligations that don't appear (fully) on the balance sheet distort leverage ratios:
- **Leases:** under IFRS 16 nearly all leases are on the balance sheet; under US GAAP (ASC 842) operating leases are too, but with different expense patterns. Older or local statements may still hide them.
- **Unconsolidated entities and joint ventures**, **guarantees** of others' debt, **purchase commitments**, **securitized receivables with recourse**.

Analysts adjust: add the present value of such obligations to debt before computing debt ratios.

## Fair value and its effects

Some assets (certain investments, derivatives) are carried at **fair value**, with gains in profit or in other comprehensive income. Volatility in profit may come from market moves, not operations: separate them.

## Comparing companies: watch the policies

| Choice | Effect when prices rise |
|---|---|
| FIFO vs LIFO (US GAAP only) | FIFO: higher profit and inventory; LIFO: higher COGS, lower taxes |
| Straight-line vs accelerated depreciation | Straight-line: higher early profit |
| Capitalizing vs expensing development costs (IFRS allows capitalizing some) | Capitalizing: higher early profit and assets |
| Revaluation of PP&E (IFRS allows) | Higher assets and equity, lower ROA |

Adjust both companies to the same policy before comparing ratios.

## Inflation and foreign currency

- **Inflation:** historical-cost assets are understated, so depreciation is too low and profit too high; ROA looks better than it is. Compare real (inflation-adjusted) growth, not nominal.
- **Foreign subsidiaries:** translating statements at changing exchange rates changes reported sales and assets even if nothing changed locally. A strong home currency shrinks foreign results. Translation gains and losses usually go to OCI (current rate method).

## Segment information

Large companies report results by **segment** (business line or region). Use it to see which parts drive growth and margins, which totals can hide.

## Try it

Company X: net income 120 (last year 100); operating cash flow 70 (last year 95); receivables up 35%; sales up 8%. What do you conclude, and which two ratios would you check next?

**Answer:** earnings quality is falling: profit up 20% while cash flow fell and receivables grew far faster than sales, a sign of aggressive revenue recognition or collection problems. Check **DSO** and the **accrual ratio** (and read the revenue recognition note).
