# Section B: short-term financing, raising capital and dividends

## Short-term financing and its true cost

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| Trade credit | "Free" until you skip a discount (previous lesson) |
| Bank line of credit | May require a **compensating balance** and a commitment fee |
| Commercial paper | Short-term unsecured notes from strong companies, usually cheaper than bank loans |
| Factoring or pledging receivables | Sell or borrow against receivables; costly but fast |

**Effective rate with a compensating balance:** borrow 100,000 at 8% with a 10% compensating balance. Usable funds 90,000; interest 8,000. Effective rate = 8,000 ÷ 90,000 = **8.9%**.

**Discounted loan** (interest taken upfront): 100,000 at 8% discounted → receive 92,000, pay 8,000 interest. Effective rate = 8,000 ÷ 92,000 = **8.7%**.

## Long-term financing

| Source | For the company | For investors |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonds** | Tax-deductible interest, no dilution; fixed obligations, covenants | Priority claim, fixed income |
| **Preferred stock** | No mandatory payment, but dividends not deductible | Fixed dividend, priority over common |
| **Common stock** | No fixed payments, permanent; dilutes control and EPS, most expensive | Residual claim, votes, highest risk |

Bond features to know: **secured vs debentures**, **subordinated**, **convertible** (lower coupon in exchange for conversion rights), **callable** (issuer can redeem early: investors demand a higher yield), **sinking fund**, **zero-coupon**, **covenants**.

**Leasing vs buying:** compare the PV of after-tax lease payments with the PV of after-tax costs of borrowing to buy (including depreciation tax shields and salvage).

## Raising equity

- **IPO** through investment bankers: **underwriting** (firm commitment: the bank buys the shares and bears the risk) or **best efforts**.
- **Rights offering:** existing shareholders can buy new shares first, protecting their ownership share.
- **Private placement:** sold to a few institutions, cheaper and faster, less liquid.

## Dividend policy

- **Residual policy:** fund all good projects first, pay out what's left. Dividends swing.
- **Stable dividend:** a steady, slowly rising dividend; most companies prefer it because cuts send bad signals.
- **Constant payout ratio:** a fixed % of earnings; dividends swing with profit.
- **Theories:** dividend irrelevance (Modigliani–Miller), bird-in-the-hand (investors prefer certain dividends), tax preference (capital gains taxed less or later), **signaling** (a dividend rise signals management's confidence), **clientele effect**.

**Important dates:** declaration date (liability created) → **ex-dividend date** (buy on or after it and you don't get the dividend; price drops by about the dividend) → record date → payment date.

**Stock dividends and splits** don't change total equity or the company's value; they change the number of shares and the price per share. **Share repurchases** return cash like dividends, can raise EPS, and signal that management thinks shares are cheap.

## Residual policy example

Net income 5,000,000; capital budget 6,000,000; target structure 40% debt, 60% equity. Equity needed = 3,600,000. Dividends = 5,000,000 − 3,600,000 = **1,400,000**.

## Try it

(a) A 200,000 loan at 9% requires a 15% compensating balance (the company keeps no balance now). Effective rate? (b) Under a residual policy, net income 2,000,000, budget 2,500,000, equity weight 70%: dividends?

**Answers:** (a) 18,000 ÷ 170,000 = **10.6%**. (b) 2,000,000 − 1,750,000 = **250,000**.
