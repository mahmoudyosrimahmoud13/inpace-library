# Read: Chapter 9, Consistency and Consensus

The longest and hardest chapter. Its message: having seen everything that can go wrong (Chapter 8), find general
abstractions with useful guarantees, implement them once, and let applications rely on them. The most important is
**consensus**: getting nodes to agree. Plan two days: stop after *Ordering Guarantees* on the first.

## Consistency guarantees

Eventual consistency is weak ("convergence, eventually"). This chapter looks at stronger models, and their cost.

## Linearizability

- **What makes a system linearizable?** Make it look as if there were only one copy of the data, and every operation
  took effect at one instant. The World Cup example (Figure 9-1); the register examples (Figures 9-2 to 9-4); the
  rule that once any read returns the new value, every later read must too.
- **Linearizability versus serializability**: different things; read the box.
- **Relying on linearizability**: leader election and locks, uniqueness constraints, and **cross-channel timing
  dependencies** (the image resizer of Figure 9-5).
- **Implementing it**: which replication methods can be linearizable (single-leader maybe, consensus yes,
  multi-leader no, leaderless probably not, and why quorums don't guarantee it, Figure 9-6).
- **The cost**: the network-partition example (Figure 9-7), the **CAP theorem** and why the author finds it unhelpful,
  and the real cost of linearizability, which is **latency**.

## Ordering guarantees

- **Ordering and causality**: causal order is a partial order, linearizability a total order; **causal consistency**
  is the strongest model that stays available during network faults.
- **Sequence number ordering** and **Lamport timestamps** (Figure 9-8): a total order consistent with causality. Then
  the crucial limitation: to enforce a uniqueness constraint (a username) **now**, timestamps aren't enough, because
  you'd have to know you've seen everyone else's operations.
- **Total order broadcast**: reliable delivery in the same order to every node; how it relates to linearizable
  storage, and that it's equivalent to consensus.

## Distributed transactions and consensus

- **Atomic commit and two-phase commit** (Figure 9-9): the coordinator, prepare and commit, the two "points of no
  return", and the coordinator failure that leaves participants **in doubt** (Figure 9-10). Three-phase commit.
- **Distributed transactions in practice**: XA, and why in-doubt transactions holding locks are so harmful.
- **Fault-tolerant consensus**: its properties (uniform agreement, integrity, validity, termination); the algorithms
  (Viewstamped Replication, Paxos, Raft, Zab); **epoch numbers** (terms in Raft) and **quorums**: two rounds of voting
  that must overlap; the limits of consensus.
- **Membership and coordination services** (ZooKeeper, etcd): what they provide and what they're used for.

## After reading

Why can't a set of nodes use Lamport timestamps alone to make sure two users don't register the same username? What
does total order broadcast add?

## What to take away

- Linearizability: one copy of the data, every read sees the latest completed write. It costs latency, and
  availability during partitions.
- Lamport timestamps give a causal total order, but only after the fact.
- Consensus (and 2PC's weaker cousin) is how nodes agree; majorities and epochs prevent two leaders.
