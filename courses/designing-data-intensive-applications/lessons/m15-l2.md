# Looking back, and what to learn next

## The book in one table

| Part | Big question | Your answer, from what you built |
|---|---|---|
| I. Foundations | How does one machine store and find data, and evolve it? | Logs, hash indexes, LSM-trees and B-trees; rows versus columns; schemas with tags or resolution |
| II. Distributed data | What changes with many machines? | Replication lag, failover losses, quorums; partitioning and rebalancing; isolation anomalies; unreliable networks and clocks; consensus by majority |
| III. Derived data | How do systems of record feed everything else? | Immutable inputs and rerunnable batch jobs; logs with offsets, CDC and idempotence; one log, many views, audits |

## Questions you can now answer in a design discussion

- Is this workload read-heavy or write-heavy, and which storage engine follows from that?
- What happens to acknowledged writes if this leader dies right now?
- Which isolation level does our database actually give us, and which anomaly would hurt us?
- What's the partition key, where are the hot spots, and how do we add a node?
- What happens when this request is retried, and how do we make it safe?
- Which system is the source of truth, and how do the others stay in sync with it?

## What to learn next

- **Data engineering, the practical side**: the library's *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* course (the data
  lifecycle as one project) and *Data engineering: building production pipelines* (Kafka, Spark, dbt and BigQuery
  in practice).
- **Databases in depth**: *Advanced PostgreSQL: how Postgres really works* in the library: MVCC, WAL and B-trees in a
  real engine you can now read about with confidence.
- **Distributed systems**: read the Raft paper ("In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm") and implement a
  real Raft log replication on top of your project 11.
- **The book's references**: every chapter ends with a list of papers. Pick the three that made you most curious.

## Keep the lab growing

Each of your projects has open ends: hint files, persistent offsets, split-vote statistics, a real merging range scan.
Pick one a week. A lab that grows tells more about you than one finished in a fortnight.
