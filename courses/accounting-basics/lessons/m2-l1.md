# Every transaction touches two things

A **transaction** is any event that changes the business's assets, liabilities or equity and can be measured in money: buying, selling, paying, borrowing, the owner putting money in or taking it out.

A conversation with a customer is not a transaction. Signing a contract to deliver next month is not a transaction yet. Delivering the order is.

## The two-sided rule

Every transaction changes **at least two** items, and the equation stays balanced. This is called **duality**, and it's the reason for "double-entry" bookkeeping, which you'll meet on Day 3.

There are only a few patterns:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| One asset up, another asset down | Buy an oven with cash |
| Asset up, liability up | Buy flour on credit |
| Asset up, equity up | Owner invests cash; cash sale |
| Asset down, liability down | Pay the supplier |
| Asset down, equity down | Pay wages (an expense); owner takes cash |
| Liability up, equity down | Wages earned by staff but not paid yet |

## Nour's first month, analyzed

Here is the bakery's whole of June. Read each line and check that it balances.

| # | Date | What happened | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jun 1 | Nour invests $20,000 cash | Cash +20,000; Capital +20,000 |
| 2 | Jun 2 | Buys an oven for $8,000 cash | Equipment +8,000; Cash −8,000 |
| 3 | Jun 3 | Buys flour and supplies on credit, $1,500 | Supplies +1,500; Accounts payable +1,500 |
| 4 | Jun 4 | Pays 3 months' rent in advance, $3,000 | Prepaid rent +3,000; Cash −3,000 |
| 5 | Jun 10 | Sells cakes for cash, $2,400 | Cash +2,400; Revenue +2,400 |
| 6 | Jun 12 | Delivers a catering order to a café on credit, $1,200 | Accounts receivable +1,200; Revenue +1,200 |
| 7 | Jun 15 | Pays her assistant's wages, $900 | Wages expense +900; Cash −900 |
| 8 | Jun 20 | Pays the supplier $1,000 of what she owes | Accounts payable −1,000; Cash −1,000 |
| 9 | Jun 25 | The café pays $700 of its bill | Cash +700; Accounts receivable −700 |
| 10 | Jun 28 | Nour takes $500 for personal use | Drawings +500; Cash −500 |
| 11 | Jun 30 | Pays the electricity bill, $300 | Utilities expense +300; Cash −300 |

Some lines need a closer look:

- **Line 4:** rent paid in advance is not an expense yet. On June 4 Nour hasn't *used* any of the rent. She owns the right to use the shop for three months, so it's an **asset**, *prepaid rent*. On Day 6 we'll move one month of it to expense.
- **Line 6:** revenue is recorded when the cakes are **delivered**, not when the money arrives. The café now owes her: *accounts receivable*.
- **Line 9:** collecting the money is **not** new revenue. It was already counted on line 6. One asset (receivable) just turned into another (cash).
- **Line 10:** drawings are not a business expense. They lower equity, but they didn't help earn any revenue.

## The totals at the end of June

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash | 20,000 − 8,000 − 3,000 + 2,400 − 900 − 1,000 + 700 − 500 − 300 = **9,400** |
| Accounts receivable | 1,200 − 700 = **500** |
| Supplies | **1,500** |
| Prepaid rent | **3,000** |
| Equipment | **8,000** |
| **Total assets** | **22,400** |
| Accounts payable | 1,500 − 1,000 = **500** |
| Capital | **20,000** |
| Revenue | 2,400 + 1,200 = **3,600** |
| Expenses | 900 + 300 = **1,200** |
| Drawings | **500** |

Check: liabilities + equity = 500 + 20,000 + 3,600 − 1,200 − 500 = **22,400**. It balances.
