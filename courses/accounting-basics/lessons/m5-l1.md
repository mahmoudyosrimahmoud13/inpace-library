# The trial balance

At the end of a period (a month, usually), you list every ledger account with its balance, debits in one column and credits in the other. That list is the **trial balance**.

If the bookkeeping followed the double-entry rule, **the two columns are equal**.

Equal. The books are arithmetically in order.

## What a balanced trial balance proves, and what it doesn't

It proves that **total debits = total credits**. It does **not** prove the books are right. These mistakes still balance:

| Mistake | Example | Still balances? |
|---|---|---|
| A transaction left out completely | Forgot the electricity bill | Yes |
| Recorded twice | Wages entered twice | Yes |
| Wrong account, right type | Rent recorded as Utilities expense | Yes |
| Debit and credit swapped | Credit Cash, debit Revenue on a sale | Yes |
| Wrong amount on both sides | Sale of 420 entered as 240 | Yes |

So a balanced trial balance is a first check, not a guarantee. The real protection is good evidence (receipts, invoices) and checks like comparing your Cash account with the **bank statement** every month (*bank reconciliation*).

## Why it's still worth doing

- It catches arithmetic and posting slips quickly.
- It's the **starting point for the reports**: the income statement and balance sheet are built from it.
- It's the starting point for the **adjusting entries** on Day 6.

## The three trial balances of a period

You'll prepare it three times in each cycle:

1. **Unadjusted trial balance:** right after posting the month's transactions (the one above).
2. **Adjusted trial balance:** after the adjusting entries (Day 6).
3. **Post-closing trial balance:** after closing the books (Day 7). Only balance sheet accounts remain.

## Try it

A small shop's ledger balances are: Cash 4,200; Inventory 3,000; Equipment 6,000; Accounts payable 1,800; Loan payable 4,000; Capital 6,000; Sales 5,400; Rent expense 1,200; Wages expense 2,400; Drawings 400.

Put them in a trial balance. Does it balance?

**Answer:** Debits: 4,200 + 3,000 + 6,000 + 1,200 + 2,400 + 400 = **17,200**. Credits: 1,800 + 4,000 + 6,000 + 5,400 = **17,200**. It balances.
