# Debit and credit, without the confusion

Writing "+" and "−" next to every item works for a few transactions. Real businesses have thousands, so accountants use a fixed system: **debits and credits**.

Forget what "credit" means at your bank. In accounting:

> **Debit = left side. Credit = right side.** Nothing more.

## The T-account

Picture each account (Cash, Supplies, Revenue...) as a letter T:

```
            Cash
   Debit (left) | Credit (right)
   -------------+--------------
       20,000   |  8,000
        2,400   |  3,000
```

Amounts on the left are debits, amounts on the right are credits. The **balance** is the difference between the two sides.

## The rules

Whether a debit means "up" or "down" depends on the type of account. The rule follows the equation: assets are on the left of `=`, so they grow on the left (debit). Liabilities and equity are on the right, so they grow on the right (credit).

| Account type | Increase with | Decrease with | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assets** | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| **Expenses** | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| **Drawings** | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| **Liabilities** | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| **Equity (capital)** | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| **Revenue** | Credit | Debit | Credit |

Why do expenses and drawings increase with a debit? Because they *reduce* equity, and equity decreases with a debit.

A memory trick: **DEAD CLIC**
- **D**ebits increase **E**xpenses, **A**ssets, **D**rawings
- **C**redits increase **L**iabilities, **I**ncome (revenue), **C**apital

## The golden rule of double entry

> In every transaction, **total debits = total credits**.

That's what keeps the equation balanced, and it's why the system is called **double-entry bookkeeping**.

## Nour's transactions in debits and credits

| # | Debit | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash | Capital | 20,000 |
| 2 | Equipment | Cash | 8,000 |
| 3 | Supplies | Accounts payable | 1,500 |
| 4 | Prepaid rent | Cash | 3,000 |
| 5 | Cash | Revenue | 2,400 |
| 6 | Accounts receivable | Revenue | 1,200 |
| 7 | Wages expense | Cash | 900 |
| 8 | Accounts payable | Cash | 1,000 |
| 9 | Cash | Accounts receivable | 700 |
| 10 | Drawings | Cash | 500 |
| 11 | Utilities expense | Cash | 300 |

Check line 8: Nour pays the supplier. Cash (an asset) goes down: **credit** Cash. Accounts payable (a liability) goes down: **debit** Accounts payable.

## How to work out any entry, step by step

1. Which accounts change? (at least two)
2. What type is each? (asset, liability, equity, revenue, expense, drawings)
3. Does each go up or down?
4. Use the table: up or down, debit or credit?
5. Check: debits = credits.

## Try it

Write the debit and credit for each:

1. Buy a delivery bike for $1,000 on credit.
2. Receive a $250 internet bill, to be paid next week.
3. A customer pays $400 now for a birthday cake to be delivered next month.

**Answers:**
1. Debit Equipment 1,000 / Credit Accounts payable 1,000
2. Debit Utilities (internet) expense 250 / Credit Accounts payable 250
3. Debit Cash 400 / Credit **Unearned revenue** 400 (a liability: she owes the cake. It becomes revenue when the cake is delivered.)
