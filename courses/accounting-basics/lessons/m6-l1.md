# Cash basis or accrual basis

There are two ways to decide **when** to record revenue and expenses.

## Cash basis

Record revenue when cash comes **in**, and expenses when cash goes **out**.

Simple, and fine for a personal budget. But it can badly mislead a business:

- Nour pays three months of rent on June 4. On a cash basis, June carries all $3,000 and July and August look rent-free.
- She delivers the café's $1,200 catering order in June and gets paid in July. On a cash basis the work looks like July's.

Profit then jumps up and down depending on when bills are paid, not on how the business is really doing.

## Accrual basis

Record revenue when it's **earned**, and expenses when they're **incurred** (used up to earn revenue), whenever the cash moves.

Two principles drive it:

- **Revenue recognition:** revenue belongs to the period in which the goods or services were **delivered**.
- **Matching:** expenses belong to the same period as the revenue they helped earn. Rent for June is June's expense, whenever it's paid.

Almost all companies, and all published financial statements (under IFRS or US GAAP), use the **accrual basis**. It's what this course teaches.

## Same month, two answers

Nour's June under both methods:

| | Cash basis | Accrual basis |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Cash sales 2,400 + collected from café 700 = **3,100** | Cash sales 2,400 + catering delivered 1,200 + work done not yet billed 200 = **3,800** |
| Rent | Paid 3,000 | One month used: **1,000** |
| Supplies | Paid supplier 1,000 | Used this month: **900** |
| Oven | Paid 8,000 | One month's wear: **120** |
| Wages | Paid 900 | Earned by the assistant: **1,050** |
| Electricity | Paid 300 | **300** |
| **Profit** | 3,100 − 13,200 = **−10,100 (a big loss!)** | 3,800 − 3,370 = **430** |

On a cash basis, June looks like a disaster because Nour bought an oven and paid rent ahead. On an accrual basis, June shows a small real profit, which is the truth. (Don't worry about where 1,050, 900 and 120 come from: that's the next lesson.)

## The timing gaps that accrual creates

When cash and the "earned/incurred" moment don't happen together, a temporary balance sheet item appears:

| Situation | Item created | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Paid **before** using it | Prepaid expense (prepaid rent, insurance) | Asset |
| Used **before** paying | Accrued expense (wages payable, interest payable) | Liability |
| Cash received **before** delivering | Unearned revenue | Liability |
| Delivered **before** cash received | Accounts receivable / accrued revenue | Asset |

At the end of each period, accountants make **adjusting entries** to bring each of these up to date. That's next.

## Try it

A gym sells a 12-month membership for $600 on October 1 and is paid in full that day. Its year ends December 31.

1. On a cash basis, how much revenue is recorded this year?
2. On an accrual basis?
3. What's on the balance sheet on December 31?

**Answers:** 1. $600. 2. Three months earned: 600 × 3/12 = **$150**. 3. **Unearned revenue of $450**: the gym still owes 9 months of service.
