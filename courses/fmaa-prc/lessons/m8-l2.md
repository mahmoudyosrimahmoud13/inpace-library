# Types of Budgets and the Budgeting Process

## The Master Budget

The **master budget** is a comprehensive set of budgets that covers all aspects of the business. It has two parts:

**Operating budgets** (plan for income):
1. Sales budget — the starting point (units × price).
2. Production budget — units to produce = units to sell + ending inventory − beginning inventory.
3. Direct materials budget.
4. Direct labor budget.
5. Manufacturing overhead budget.
6. Selling and administrative expense budget.
7. Budgeted income statement.

**Financial budgets** (plan for the balance sheet and cash):
8. Capital expenditure budget.
9. Cash budget.
10. Budgeted balance sheet.

The **sales budget drives everything** — without a sales forecast, the rest of the master budget cannot be built.

## Cash Budget

The cash budget shows expected cash inflows and outflows:

```
Opening cash balance
+ Cash receipts (collections from customers, etc.)
− Cash payments (purchases, payroll, overhead, capex, taxes, debt service)
= Ending cash balance
```

If ending cash falls below the minimum required balance → plan to borrow.  
If ending cash exceeds the target → plan to invest or repay debt.

## Types of Budgets by Approach

### Static (Fixed) Budget
- Prepared for one expected activity level.
- Not adjusted as actual activity differs.
- Variance analysis can be misleading if volume differs from plan.

### Flexible Budget
- Prepared for multiple activity levels (or adjusted to actual activity).
- Separates the volume effect from the efficiency effect.
- More meaningful for controlling variable costs.

**Flexible budget variance** = Actual cost − Flexible budget cost at actual volume.

### Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)
- Every expense must be justified from zero each period.
- No automatic rollover of prior year budget.
- Time-consuming but eliminates entrenched spending.

### Rolling (Continuous) Budget
- Always covers a fixed forward period (e.g., 12 months).
- Each month, drop the month just completed and add a new month.
- Keeps planning current; prevents the "December rush."

### Activity-Based Budgeting (ABB)
- Budget driven by activity drivers, not just expense categories.
- More granular and accurate for overhead-heavy organizations.

## Behavioral Issues in Budgeting

**Budgetary slack (padding)** — managers intentionally underestimate revenues or overestimate costs to make targets easier to hit. Leads to biased budgets.

**Goal congruence** — the budget should align individual managers' goals with the organization's goals. Poor budget design can create perverse incentives (e.g., spending all budget at year-end to avoid cuts next year).

**Participative budgeting** — involving budget holders in the process increases accuracy and buy-in, but also creates opportunities for padding.

## Capital Budgeting (Overview)

Capital budgeting evaluates long-term investment decisions (equipment, plants, acquisitions). Key methods:

| Method | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Payback period** | Years to recover the initial investment |
| **Net present value (NPV)** | PV of cash inflows − initial investment; accept if NPV > 0 |
| **Internal rate of return (IRR)** | Discount rate that makes NPV = 0; accept if IRR > cost of capital |
| **Accounting rate of return (ARR)** | Average annual income ÷ average investment |

> **Exam tip:** Know the master budget sequence — it starts with the **sales budget** and ends with the **budgeted balance sheet**. Everything flows from the sales forecast.
