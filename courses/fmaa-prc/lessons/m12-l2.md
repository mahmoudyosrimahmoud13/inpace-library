# Types of Business Fraud and Ethical Violations

## What Is Fraud?

**Fraud** is an intentional act of deception for financial gain or to cause loss to another party. Unlike an honest error, fraud requires **intent**.

The classic fraud definition: a false representation of fact, made knowingly or recklessly, intended to be acted upon, that causes actual damage.

---

## The Fraud Triangle

Three conditions must be present for fraud to occur:

1. **Pressure (Incentive)** — a personal or organizational need: financial pressure, performance targets, bonuses tied to results.
2. **Opportunity** — a weakness in controls that makes fraud possible without detection.
3. **Rationalization** — the fraudster convinces themselves it is acceptable: "I'll pay it back," "They owe me," "Everyone does it."

Eliminating or reducing any one element reduces fraud risk.

---

## Categories of Fraud

### Financial Statement Fraud

Intentional misrepresentation of financial data to mislead stakeholders.

**Common methods:**
- **Inflating revenues** — recording fictitious sales, recognizing revenue prematurely.
- **Understating expenses** — capitalizing operating costs, failing to record liabilities.
- **Overstating assets** — inflating inventory values, failing to write off bad debts.
- **Window dressing** — timing transactions to make year-end statements look better than they are.

Motivation: bonuses based on earnings, meeting analyst expectations, maintaining stock price, satisfying loan covenants.

### Asset Misappropriation (Theft)

The most common type of fraud. Employees steal cash or assets from the organization.

**Examples:**
- **Skimming** — stealing cash before it is recorded in the books.
- **Larceny** — stealing cash after it has been recorded.
- **Payroll fraud** — ghost employees (fictitious employees on payroll), inflated hours.
- **Expense reimbursement fraud** — claiming personal expenses as business expenses.
- **Inventory theft** — stealing goods, falsifying inventory records.

### Corruption

Employees abuse their position or authority for personal benefit.

**Examples:**
- **Bribery** — paying or receiving something of value to influence a business decision.
- **Kickbacks** — a supplier pays an employee (buyer) a portion of the contract value.
- **Conflicts of interest** — an employee has an undisclosed personal interest in a transaction.
- **Bid rigging** — colluding to manipulate a competitive bidding process.

---

## Internal Control and Fraud Prevention

**Key controls to prevent fraud:**

- **Segregation of duties** — no single person controls all phases of a transaction (authorization, custody, recording).
- **Authorization limits** — require approval for transactions above a threshold.
- **Independent reconciliations** — bank statements, inventory counts reviewed by someone who doesn't handle cash or inventory.
- **Whistleblower hotlines** — anonymous reporting mechanisms.
- **Background checks** — especially for employees with access to assets or sensitive data.
- **Mandatory vacations** — fraud sometimes requires the fraudster to be present continuously; mandatory leave exposes it.
- **Job rotation** — rotating employees across roles exposes irregularities.

---

## Money Laundering

**Money laundering** is the process of making illegally obtained money appear legitimate.

**Three stages:**
1. **Placement** — introducing illegal funds into the financial system (e.g., depositing cash in small amounts, mixing with business receipts).
2. **Layering** — moving funds through multiple transactions to obscure the trail (wire transfers, offshore accounts, multiple conversions).
3. **Integration** — the funds re-enter the economy as seemingly legitimate (real estate purchase, investing in a business).

Businesses have legal obligations to detect and report suspicious activity (AML — Anti-Money Laundering rules).

---

## Whistleblowing

A **whistleblower** reports wrongdoing within an organization to internal management or external authorities.

**Protections vary** by jurisdiction and circumstance. Generally:
- Internal reporting is preferred first.
- External reporting (to regulators) may be protected if internal channels fail or the matter is serious.

Ethical employees have a professional and sometimes legal duty to report fraud.

## Common Exam Scenarios

The FMAA tests practical scenario judgment:

| Scenario | Issue |
|---|---|
| A manager requests that you book revenue now even though the goods haven't been delivered | Revenue recognition fraud / Ethics: Integrity |
| A supplier representative sends you a gift worth $500 before contract renewal | Potential bribe / Conflict of interest |
| An employee handles both payroll data entry and payroll checks | Segregation of duties failure |
| CFO tells you to reclassify a large operating expense as a capital expenditure | Expense manipulation / Financial statement fraud |
| A colleague asks you to sign off on work you have not reviewed | Competence / Credibility standard |

> **Exam tip:** The FMAA tests both fraud identification and what to do about it. Know the fraud triangle, the three fraud categories (financial statement fraud, asset misappropriation, corruption), and the IMA's four ethical standards — which one is violated tells you how to frame your answer.
