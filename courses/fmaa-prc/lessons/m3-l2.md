# Internal Control Systems and Safeguarding

## What Is Internal Control?

Internal control is any action taken by management, the board, and other parties to **manage risk and increase the likelihood** that established objectives and goals will be achieved.

Controls exist to:
- Safeguard assets from theft, error, or misuse.
- Ensure the accuracy and reliability of financial records.
- Promote operational efficiency.
- Ensure compliance with laws and regulations.

## COSO Framework — Five Components

The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations (COSO) framework is the most widely used model:

1. **Control environment** — the "tone at the top." Culture, integrity, ethical values, competence, and management's commitment to controls.
2. **Risk assessment** — identifying and analyzing risks that may prevent the company from meeting its objectives.
3. **Control activities** — the actual policies and procedures that address risks (authorizations, reconciliations, segregation of duties, etc.).
4. **Information and communication** — relevant, reliable information must flow throughout the organization.
5. **Monitoring** — ongoing evaluation of the effectiveness of the control system.

## Key Control Principles

### Segregation of Duties
No single person should have control over an entire transaction from start to finish. Split the functions of:
- **Authorization** — approving a transaction.
- **Recording** — entering it into the books.
- **Custody** — physically handling the asset.

**Example violation:** The cashier who handles cash also records cash receipts. This allows theft to be hidden.

### Physical Controls
- Locks, safes, and secure areas for cash and inventory.
- Access badges and security cameras.
- Inventory counts and surprise audits.

### Authorization Controls
- Requiring manager approval for purchases above a threshold.
- Dual signatures for large checks.

### Reconciliations
- Regular bank reconciliations to compare the company's records with the bank statement.
- Reconciling accounts receivable sub-ledger to the general ledger.

### IT Access Controls
- Passwords, multi-factor authentication.
- Role-based access — employees can only access systems relevant to their job.
- Audit logs of system activity.

## Limitations of Internal Control

No system is perfect:
- Controls can be overridden by management (management override).
- Collusion between employees can defeat segregation of duties.
- Controls cost money — there is a cost-benefit tradeoff.
- Human error.

## Just-In-Time (JIT) and Internal Control

JIT manufacturing produces units only when they are needed in the next production step. Benefits for internal control:
- Reduces inventory levels (and thus the risk of inventory theft or obsolescence).
- Reduces carrying costs.
- Requires tight supplier relationships.

**Risk:** JIT leaves little buffer — a supply chain disruption halts production immediately.

## Carrying Costs vs. Ordering Costs

**Carrying costs** (holding costs) of inventory include: storage, insurance, opportunity cost of capital tied up, and risk of obsolescence.

**Ordering costs** include: placing orders, receiving and inspecting goods, and administrative processing.

The economic order quantity (EOQ) balances these two to minimize total inventory cost.

> **Exam tip:** Segregation of duties is the most tested internal control concept. Know what it is, why it matters, and what a *violation* looks like.
