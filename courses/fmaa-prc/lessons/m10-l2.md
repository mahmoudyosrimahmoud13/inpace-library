# Overhead Allocation Methods

## Why Allocate Overhead?

Products cannot be sold without overhead (the factory needs heat, supervision, and maintenance). Overhead must be allocated to products to:
- Determine the full cost of a product.
- Price products to cover all costs.
- Value inventory correctly on the balance sheet.
- Evaluate product profitability accurately.

---

## Traditional Allocation Methods

### Single Plant-Wide Rate

**Step 1:** Accumulate all factory overhead into one pool.  
**Step 2:** Choose one allocation base (usually direct labor hours).  
**Step 3:** Rate = Total overhead ÷ Total DLH.

**Weakness:** Assumes all overhead is driven by the same base. This distorts costs when:
- Some products are labor-intensive; others are machine-intensive.
- Different departments have very different overhead structures.

### Departmental Rates

**Step 1:** Accumulate overhead by department.  
**Step 2:** Choose an appropriate base for each department (DLH for labor-intensive, MH for automated).  
**Step 3:** Rate per department = Departmental overhead ÷ Departmental activity.

More accurate than a plant-wide rate when departments differ significantly.

### Activity-Based Costing (ABC) — Detailed Allocation

ABC assigns overhead based on **activities** that actually cause costs.

**Four steps:**
1. **Identify activities** (setups, inspections, material handling, machining).
2. **Assign costs to activity cost pools** — group overhead costs by activity.
3. **Calculate a cost driver rate** per activity:  
   Rate = Activity cost pool ÷ Total cost driver units
4. **Allocate to products** based on how much of each activity each product consumes.

**Example:**

| Activity | Total Cost | Driver | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine setups | $60,000 | 200 setups | $300 per setup |
| Quality inspections | $40,000 | 1,000 inspections | $40 per inspection |
| Material handling | $30,000 | 500 moves | $60 per move |

Product X uses 50 setups, 200 inspections, 100 moves:  
Overhead allocated = (50 × $300) + (200 × $40) + (100 × $60) = $15,000 + $8,000 + $6,000 = **$29,000**

## Support Department Cost Allocation

Factories have both **production departments** (machining, assembly) and **support departments** (maintenance, HR, cafeteria). Support department costs must be allocated to production departments before being allocated to products.

**Three methods:**

1. **Direct method** — allocate support department costs directly to production departments only. Simple but ignores services that support departments provide to each other.

2. **Step-down method** — allocate in a sequence: the support department that provides the most service to others goes first. Partial recognition of inter-department services.

3. **Reciprocal method** — mathematically accounts for all services between support departments. Most accurate but complex.

---

## Over- and Under-Applied Overhead Revisited

If production is **higher than budgeted:** More units produced → more overhead applied → likely **over-applied** (applied > actual).

If production is **lower than budgeted:** Fewer units → less overhead applied → likely **under-applied** (applied < actual).

**Disposition:**
- Simple approach: add under-applied to COGS (or subtract over-applied from COGS).
- More precise: prorate among WIP, Finished Goods, and COGS.

> **Exam tip:** For the FMAA, understand the logic of cost allocation — why overhead must be allocated, what a "cost driver" is, and how ABC differs from traditional costing (more drivers, more accuracy).
