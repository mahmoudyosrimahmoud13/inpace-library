# Standard Costs and Product Costing Systems

## Standard Costs

**Standard costs** are predetermined costs — what a product *should* cost under normal, efficient operating conditions.

Standard costs are set for:
- **Direct materials:** standard quantity × standard price per unit.
- **Direct labor:** standard hours × standard wage rate.
- **Overhead:** standard allocation rate × standard activity.

### Benefits of Standard Costing
- Simplifies bookkeeping (record one standard cost rather than tracking actual costs each time).
- Enables variance analysis — compare standard to actual to identify inefficiencies.
- Provides targets for performance evaluation.

### Standard Cost Variances

**Materials variances:**
- **Price variance** = (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity purchased.
- **Quantity (usage) variance** = (Actual quantity used − Standard quantity allowed) × Standard price.

**Labor variances:**
- **Rate variance** = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours.
- **Efficiency variance** = (Actual hours − Standard hours allowed) × Standard rate.

**Favorable** = actual < standard (cost is less than expected).  
**Unfavorable** = actual > standard (cost is more than expected).

## Product Costing Systems

### Job Order Costing
Used when products/services are **distinct and customized** (construction projects, custom furniture, audit engagements).

- Costs are tracked on a **job cost sheet** for each job.
- Direct materials and direct labor are traced directly.
- Overhead is **allocated** using a predetermined overhead rate.

**Predetermined overhead rate** = Budgeted overhead ÷ Budgeted activity level (e.g., direct labor hours).

### Process Costing
Used when **identical units are produced in continuous processes** (chemicals, beverages, cement, oil refining).

- Costs are accumulated by **department** (process) for a period.
- Cost per unit = Total department costs ÷ Equivalent units produced.
- **Equivalent units** account for partially completed units (WIP).

### Activity-Based Costing (ABC)
Allocates overhead based on the **activities that drive costs**, rather than a single volume-based driver.

1. Identify activities (machining, setups, inspections, purchasing).
2. Assign overhead costs to activity cost pools.
3. Calculate a cost driver rate for each activity.
4. Allocate costs to products based on each product's consumption of activities.

**ABC is more accurate** but more complex than traditional costing. It is particularly valuable when overhead is large and products consume overhead differently.

### Actual vs. Normal Costing

| | Actual Costing | Normal Costing |
|---|---|---|
| DM and DL | Actual | Actual |
| Overhead | Actual | Predetermined rate × Actual activity |
| Use | Exact but delayed | Timely; most common |

**Normal costing** is the most common because actual overhead rates are only known at year-end. Using a predetermined rate allows timely product costing throughout the year.

> **Exam tip:** Know when to use job order vs. process costing. Job order = distinct products. Process costing = mass production of identical units.
