# Profitability Ratios and Putting It All Together

## Profitability Ratios

Measure the ability to generate **income relative to sales, assets, or equity**.

| Ratio | Formula | What It Tells You |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross profit margin** | Gross profit ÷ Net sales | % of revenue remaining after direct costs |
| **Operating profit margin** | Operating income ÷ Net sales | % of revenue from core operations |
| **Net profit margin** | Net income ÷ Net sales | % of revenue remaining as profit |
| **Return on assets (ROA)** | Net income ÷ Average total assets | Profit per dollar of assets |
| **Return on equity (ROE)** | Net income ÷ Average equity | Profit per dollar of owners' investment |

### Interpreting Profitability

- **Gross margin** falls when COGS rises faster than revenue (rising input costs, pricing pressure).
- **Operating margin** falls further when SG&A or other operating costs also rise.
- **Net margin** is the bottom line after taxes and interest.
- **ROA** measures management's ability to use assets to generate profit.
- **ROE** is higher than ROA when the company uses financial leverage — debt amplifies returns to equity holders.

**ROE > ROA** is the normal relationship when debt is in the capital structure.

---

## DuPont Analysis

ROE can be decomposed into three drivers:

**ROE = Net profit margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier**

= (Net income / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)

This reveals *why* ROE changed:
- Margin improvement (efficiency in converting sales to profit)?
- Asset turnover improvement (using assets more efficiently)?
- Increased leverage (equity multiplier)?

## Ratio Analysis — Pulling It All Together

No single ratio tells the complete story. Use them as a system:

| Analysis Goal | Key Ratios |
|---|---|
| Short-term survival | Current ratio, quick ratio, cash ratio |
| Long-term solvency | Debt to equity, TIE |
| Operational efficiency | Asset turnover, A/R days, inventory days |
| Profitability | Gross margin, operating margin, ROA, ROE |

### Common Exam Scenarios

**Scenario 1:** A company's current ratio is 2.3 before it buys inventory on credit.
- Both current assets and current liabilities rise by the same amount.
- Because the ratio > 1, the new ratio will be **lower**.

**Scenario 2:** A company collects a large receivable in cash.
- Current assets do not change (A/R down, cash up — both are current assets).
- Current ratio **unchanged**.

**Scenario 3:** A company's days sales in receivables increases year over year.
- Customers are taking longer to pay.
- May signal credit quality problems or relaxed collection policies.

## Cash Management Ratios

**Motives for holding cash:**
- **Transaction** — to pay normal business obligations.
- **Precautionary** — buffer against unexpected needs.
- **Speculative** — to take advantage of unexpected investment opportunities.

A company holding cash for a **speculative motive** is holding it to take advantage of bargain purchases or investment opportunities, not for routine payments.

---

## Net Working Capital

**Net working capital (NWC)** = Current assets − Current liabilities

Positive NWC = short-term assets exceed short-term obligations → financial cushion.

**NWC ratio** = NWC ÷ Total assets — shows NWC as a proportion of the total asset base.

> **Exam tip:** Profitability ratios use **average** balance sheet values in the denominator (average assets, average equity). If the exam gives you only one balance sheet date, use that figure.
