# Capstone: read a real annual report

Time to use everything on a real company. Use the annual report you found on Day 1 (or pick one now: a listed bank, telecom, food, retail or industrial company in your country, or a well-known international one).

Work in a spreadsheet. Take your time: an experienced analyst spends hours on this, not minutes.

## Step 1: Before the numbers (15 minutes)

- What does the company sell, to whom, and where?
- What currency and units are the statements in? Consolidated or company alone?
- What does the **auditor's report** say? Is the opinion clean ("present fairly, in all material respects")? Any emphasis of matter or key audit matters?
- Read the CEO's letter: write down the three things management says went well and any problems it mentions.

## Step 2: Copy the key numbers (30 minutes)

For the last two years (three to five if you can), copy:

| Income statement | Balance sheet | Cash flow |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Cash | Cash from operations |
| Gross profit (if shown) | Receivables | Capital spending |
| Operating profit | Inventory | Dividends paid |
| Interest expense | Total current assets | Debt raised / repaid |
| Net profit | Total assets | |
| | Total current liabilities | |
| | Total debt (loans, bonds, leases) | |
| | Total equity | |

## Step 3: Calculate (30 minutes)

At least these, for each year:

- Revenue growth
- Gross, operating and net margins
- ROE and ROA
- Current and quick ratios
- Debt to equity, interest coverage
- Receivables days and inventory days (if they apply)
- Operating cash ÷ net profit, and free cash flow

## Step 4: Read the notes behind the biggest numbers (30 minutes)

Pick the three largest balance sheet items and find their notes. Look for:

- Accounting policies (revenue recognition, inventory method, depreciation lives)
- When the debt has to be repaid, and at what interest
- Provisions and contingent liabilities (lawsuits, guarantees)
- Events after the balance sheet date

## Step 5: Tell the story (30 minutes)

Write **one page** answering:

1. Is the company **profitable**, and is it getting better or worse? Why?
2. Is it **safe**: can it pay its short-term bills and its debt?
3. Does its profit turn into **cash**?
4. What are the **two biggest risks** you found?
5. If you were a bank, would you lend to it? If you were an investor, what would you want to ask management?

## Checklist of red flags

- Receivables or inventory growing much faster than sales
- Operating cash flow well below net profit for several years
- Debt rising while profit falls
- Interest coverage below 2 to 3
- Big one-off gains propping up profit
- A qualified audit opinion, or a going concern warning
- Frequent changes in accounting policies or estimates
