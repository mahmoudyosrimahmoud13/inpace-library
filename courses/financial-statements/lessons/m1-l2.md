# Who reads the statements, and how to find them

## The readers and what they look at first

| Reader | Looks first at | Because |
|---|---|---|
| **Bank lending money** | Cash flow from operations, current ratio, debt | Can they pay interest and repay? |
| **Investor** | Profit trend, margins, return on equity | Is the business getting better, and is it worth the price? |
| **Supplier giving credit** | Current assets vs current liabilities, how fast they pay | Will I get paid? |
| **Manager** | Margins by product, costs, cash | Where to act |
| **Tax authority** | Profit before tax and its adjustments | How much tax is due |

Same statements, different questions. Good analysis always starts by asking **who you are and what you need to decide**.

## Where to find real statements

- **Listed companies** publish an **annual report** every year (and shorter quarterly reports). It's on the company's website under "Investor relations", and on the stock exchange's website (the Egyptian Exchange, Tadawul in Saudi Arabia, the SEC's EDGAR in the US, Companies House in the UK).
- **Small private companies** usually only file statements with the tax authority or a registry. You see them when you work there, lend to them or buy them.

## What's in an annual report

1. **Chair's or CEO's letter.** The story management wants to tell. Read it, but check it against the numbers.
2. **Management discussion and analysis (MD&A).** Management explains the results, risks and plans.
3. **The auditor's report.** An independent accountant says whether the statements are fair. Look for the words "**present fairly, in all material respects**" (a clean, *unqualified* opinion). Words like "except for", "adverse" or "material uncertainty related to going concern" are red flags.
4. **The four statements.**
5. **The notes.** Accounting policies, details of each big number, debts and their dates, lawsuits, events after year end.

## Reading habits that pay off

- **Always compare.** One year alone means little. Statements show the previous year next to this one for a reason. Look at five years if you can.
- **Read the headings.** Units ("in thousands", "in millions"), currency, the period, whether it's the company alone or the *consolidated* group.
- **Follow the big numbers into the notes.** If receivables doubled while sales rose 10%, the note on receivables will say why (or won't, which also tells you something).
- **Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact.** Profit depends on estimates (depreciation, bad debts, inventory). Cash is harder to dress up. Compare them.

## The materiality idea

Accountants don't chase every cent. An item is **material** if leaving it out or getting it wrong could change a reader's decision. A 500 error in a company with 490,000 of sales doesn't matter; the same error in a business with 3,000 of sales does.

## Going concern

Statements are prepared assuming the business **will keep operating** for the foreseeable future. That's why the oven is shown at cost less depreciation, not at its scrap price. If there's serious doubt (big losses, debts it can't pay), the auditor must say so.

## Try it

Pick a listed company you know (a bank, a telecom, a food company). Find its latest annual report and write down: the auditor's opinion, the units used, total revenue, net profit and total assets for the last two years. You'll use it on Day 8.
