# Receivables and bad debts

Selling on credit brings more customers, but some will pay late and a few never. Accounting has to show receivables at the amount the company **actually expects to collect**.

## The allowance method

At the end of each period, estimate how much of the receivables won't be collected, and set up an **allowance for doubtful accounts** (a contra-asset):

| Balance sheet | |
|---|---|
| Accounts receivable | 46,500 |
| Less: allowance for doubtful accounts | (1,500) |
| **Accounts receivable, net** | **45,000** |

The expense goes into this year's income statement, matching the cost of bad customers with the sales made to them.

## Estimating with an aging schedule

Sort receivables by how long they've been unpaid. The older the debt, the less likely it is to be paid:

| Age | Amount | Expected not to pay | Allowance needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not yet due (0–30 days) | 30,000 | 1% | 300 |
| 31–60 days | 10,000 | 5% | 500 |
| 61–90 days | 4,500 | 10% | 450 |
| Over 90 days | 2,000 | 12.5% | 250 |
| **Total** | **46,500** | | **1,500** |

The allowance must stand at **1,500** at year end. If it was 900 before adjusting, the adjustment is 600:

| Dec 31 | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Bad debt expense | 600 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Allowance for doubtful accounts | | 600 |

Another common way is a **percentage of credit sales** (for example 0.5% of the year's credit sales goes to bad debt expense). The aging method is more accurate because it looks at who actually owes what. IFRS 9 calls the result **expected credit losses**.

## Writing off a customer

When a specific customer is clearly never going to pay (bankrupt, disappeared), remove the debt:

| | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Allowance for doubtful accounts | 800 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Accounts receivable, Customer X | | 800 |

No expense here: it was already expensed when the allowance was set up. Net receivables don't change (both the receivable and the allowance fall by 800).

If the customer later pays after all, reverse the write-off and record the cash.

## Why not just expense it when it happens?

That's the **direct write-off method**: no allowance, expense the debt when it's written off. It's simple, but it puts the expense in the wrong year (often the year after the sale) and overstates receivables meanwhile. It's not allowed in published statements when bad debts are significant. Small businesses sometimes use it.

## How fast customers pay

> **Receivables turnover = Net sales ÷ Average receivables**
> **Days sales outstanding (DSO) = 365 ÷ Receivables turnover**

Green Valley: average receivables = (38,000 + 45,000) ÷ 2 = 41,500. Turnover = 490,000 ÷ 41,500 = **11.8 times**. DSO = 365 ÷ 11.8 = **about 31 days**.

If its terms are "pay within 30 days", customers are paying roughly on time. A DSO creeping up year after year is an early warning.

## Try it

Receivables at year end are 80,000. An aging schedule says 3,200 won't be collected. The allowance currently has 1,000. Write the adjusting entry and show net receivables.

**Answer:** Debit Bad debt expense **2,200** / Credit Allowance 2,200. Net receivables = 80,000 − 3,200 = **76,800**.
