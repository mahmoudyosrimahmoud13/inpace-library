# Inventory and the cost of goods sold

For a shop like Green Valley, inventory is the biggest current asset and COGS the biggest expense. Getting them right is getting profit right.

## The core formula

Goods **available** to sell are what you started with plus what you bought. Whatever isn't on the shelf at the end was **sold** (or lost):

> **COGS = Beginning inventory + Purchases − Ending inventory**

Green Valley 2025:

| | |
|---|---|
| Beginning inventory (1 Jan) | 52,000 |
| Add: purchases (net of returns and discounts, plus freight in) | 302,000 |
| **Goods available for sale** | **354,000** |
| Less: ending inventory (31 Dec, counted) | (60,000) |
| **Cost of goods sold** | **294,000** |

Notice the trap: a **mistake in ending inventory** changes profit one for one. If the count missed 5,000 of stock, COGS would be 5,000 too high and profit 5,000 too low.

## What goes into the cost of inventory

Everything needed to bring goods to the shelf, ready to sell: the purchase price, **freight in** (shipping to you), import duties, and handling. Minus discounts received from suppliers. Shipping goods **out** to customers is a selling expense, not inventory.

## Two ways to keep track

**Periodic system:** purchases are recorded in a Purchases account; inventory is counted only at the end of the period and COGS is worked out with the formula above. Common in small shops without scanners.

**Perpetual system:** every sale updates inventory and COGS at once (point-of-sale systems and accounting software do this). You always know what should be on the shelf. A yearly count is still needed to find **shrinkage** (theft, breakage, errors):

| | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Sale, perpetual: | Cash or Receivable 150 | Sales revenue 150 |
| | Cost of goods sold 90 | Inventory 90 |

Two entries per sale: one for the selling price, one for the cost.

## Lower of cost and net realizable value

If goods can only be sold for less than they cost (damaged, out of fashion, expired), inventory must be **written down** to what it can be sold for, minus the costs to sell it. The loss is recognized now, not when the goods are finally sold.

Example: 100 phone cases cost 8 each. A new phone model makes them hard to sell; they'll go for 5 each with 0.50 of selling costs. Net realizable value = 4.50. Write-down = 100 × (8 − 4.50) = **350**.

## Purchase discounts and returns

- **Returns to suppliers** reduce purchases.
- **Early payment discounts** (for example "2/10, net 30": 2% off if paid within 10 days, otherwise full amount in 30) reduce the cost of the goods when taken. Taking them is usually smart: 2% for paying 20 days early is like 36% a year.

## Try it

Beginning inventory 20,000; purchases 110,000; freight in 3,000; purchase returns 2,000; ending inventory 25,000. What is COGS?

**Answer:** available = 20,000 + 110,000 + 3,000 − 2,000 = 131,000. COGS = 131,000 − 25,000 = **106,000**.
