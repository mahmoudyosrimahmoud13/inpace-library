# Building cash from operations: the indirect method

There are two ways to show operating cash:

- **Direct method:** list the actual cash received and paid (from customers, to suppliers, to employees...). Easy to read, but needs detailed records.
- **Indirect method:** start from **net profit** and adjust it for everything that isn't cash. Almost every company uses this, so it's the one to master.

## The logic

Net profit is computed on the accrual basis. To get cash:

1. **Add back non-cash expenses** (depreciation and amortization took profit down but no cash left).
2. **Remove non-cash gains**, add non-cash losses (a gain on selling equipment isn't operating cash; the whole sale price goes in investing).
3. **Adjust for changes in working capital:**

| If this... | ...then cash is | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Current **asset** goes **up** (receivables, inventory, prepaids) | **lower** than profit: subtract the increase | Money got tied up |
| Current **asset** goes **down** | higher: add the decrease | Money was released |
| Current **liability** goes **up** (payables, accruals, tax payable) | **higher** than profit: add the increase | You used something without paying yet |
| Current **liability** goes **down** | lower: subtract the decrease | You paid off old bills |

A memory trick: **assets opposite, liabilities same**.

(Loans and dividends payable are financing, not working capital: they're left out here.)

## Green Valley, step by step

| Item | 2024 | 2025 | Change | Effect on cash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accounts receivable | 38,000 | 45,000 | +7,000 | **−7,000** |
| Inventory | 52,000 | 60,000 | +8,000 | **−8,000** |
| Prepaid expenses | 4,000 | 5,000 | +1,000 | **−1,000** |
| Accounts payable | 28,000 | 32,000 | +4,000 | **+4,000** |
| Accrued expenses | 5,000 | 6,000 | +1,000 | **+1,000** |
| Income tax payable | 3,000 | 4,000 | +1,000 | **+1,000** |

**Cash flows from operating activities**

| | |
|---|---|
| Net profit | 56,000 |
| Add: depreciation | 10,000 |
| Increase in accounts receivable | (7,000) |
| Increase in inventory | (8,000) |
| Increase in prepaid expenses | (1,000) |
| Increase in accounts payable | 4,000 |
| Increase in accrued expenses | 1,000 |
| Increase in income tax payable | 1,000 |
| **Net cash from operating activities** | **56,000** |

## The other two sections, from the balance sheet

- **Equipment at cost** rose from 100,000 to 120,000, with no sales: **20,000 bought** (investing). Accumulated depreciation rose 10,000, matching depreciation expense, which confirms no equipment was sold.
- **The loan** fell from 60,000 to 50,000: **10,000 repaid** (financing).
- **Retained earnings:** 8,000 + 56,000 − X = 56,000, so **dividends were 8,000** (financing).

Total: 56,000 − 20,000 − 18,000 = **18,000**, exactly the increase in cash. That's the check that tells you it's right.

## A gain on sale, if there had been one

Suppose Green Valley had sold old equipment for 5,000 with a book value of 3,000 (gain 2,000):
- Operating section: **subtract the 2,000 gain** (it's in net profit but isn't operating cash).
- Investing section: **+5,000** proceeds from sale.

## Try it

Net profit 40,000; depreciation 12,000; receivables up 6,000; inventory down 3,000; payables down 5,000; accrued expenses up 2,000. What is cash from operations?

**Answer:** 40,000 + 12,000 − 6,000 + 3,000 − 5,000 + 2,000 = **46,000**.
