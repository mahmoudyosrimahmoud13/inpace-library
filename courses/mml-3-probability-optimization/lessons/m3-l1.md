# Read: summary statistics and independence (6.4)

**Means and covariances (6.4.1)**

- **Expected value** E[g(X)] = Σ g(x)p(x) or ∫ g(x)p(x) dx. It's **linear**: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y], always.
- **Variance** V[X] = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ². **Covariance** Cov[X, Y] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]. For a random vector, the
  **covariance matrix** Σ is symmetric positive semidefinite (course 2's spectral theorem applies).
- **Correlation** = covariance divided by both standard deviations: between −1 and 1.

**Empirical versions (6.4.2)**: sample mean and sample covariance from data. These are what you actually compute.

**Three ways to write the variance (6.4.3)**, including the pairwise-differences one. The "raw-score" formula
E[X²] − E[X]² can lose precision on a computer when numbers are large.

**Sums and transformations (6.4.4)**: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]; V[X + Y] = V[X] + V[Y] + 2Cov[X, Y].
For y = Ax + b: E[y] = Aμ + b and **V[y] = AΣAᵀ** (this one is used everywhere).

**Independence (6.4.5)**: p(x, y) = p(x)p(y). Independent ⇒ uncorrelated, but **not** the other way round (the book's
y = x² example). Conditional independence, written X ⫫ Y | Z, is the idea behind graphical models (chapter 8).

**Inner products of random variables (6.4.6)**: covariance behaves like an inner product, and the variance of a sum
looks like the law of cosines. A nice link back to chapter 3.
