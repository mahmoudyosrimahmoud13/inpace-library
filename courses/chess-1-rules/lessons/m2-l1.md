# Special moves: castling, en passant and promotion

## Castling

Castling is the only move where you move two pieces at once: the **king** and a **rook**. The king moves **two squares** toward the rook, and the rook jumps to the square the king crossed.

- **Kingside castling** (short): king e1 to g1, rook h1 to f1. Written **O-O**.
- **Queenside castling** (long): king e1 to c1, rook a1 to d1. Written **O-O-O**.

```
8  r . . . k . . r
7  p p p q . p p p
6  . . n p b n . .
5  . . b . p . . .
4  . . B . P . . .
3  . . N P B N . .
2  P P P Q . P P P
1  R . . . K . . R
   a b c d e f g h
```

*White to move.* [Open on Lichess](https://lichess.org/analysis/standard/r3k2r/pppq1ppp/2npbn2/2b1p3/2B1P3/2NPBN2/PPPQ1PPP/R3K2R_w_KQkq_-_0_1)

Here both sides can castle either way. Castling does two jobs: it puts the king in a safe corner behind pawns, and it brings a rook toward the center.

You **cannot** castle if:

- the king or that rook has already moved (even if it moved back);
- there is any piece between the king and the rook;
- the king is **in check** right now;
- the king would **pass through** or **land on** a square attacked by the enemy.

To castle on a real board, move the king first, then the rook.

## En passant

A pawn that moves two squares from its start can sometimes be captured as if it had moved only one. This is **en passant** (French for "in passing").

```
8  . . . . k . . .
7  . . . p . . . .
6  . . . . . . . .
5  . . . . P . . .
4  . . . . . . . .
3  . . . . . . . .
2  . . . . . . . .
1  . . . . K . . .
   a b c d e f g h
```

*Black to move.* [Open on Lichess](https://lichess.org/analysis/standard/4k3/3p4/8/4P3/8/8/8/4K3_b_-_-_0_1)

The black pawn jumps from d7 to d5, landing right next to the white pawn on e5. On the **very next move only**, White may capture it by moving to d6, the square the pawn passed over. The pawn on d5 is removed. If White waits one move, the chance is gone.

En passant only happens between pawns, and only right after a two-square pawn move.

## Promotion

A pawn that reaches the last rank (the 8th for White, the 1st for Black) must **change into** a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same color. Almost always you choose a queen. You can have two or more queens this way.

```
8  . . . . k . . .
7  . P . . . . . .
6  . . . . . . . .
5  . . . . . . . .
4  . . . . . . . .
3  . . . . . . . .
2  . . . . . . . .
1  . . . . K . . .
   a b c d e f g h
```

*White to move.* [Open on Lichess](https://lichess.org/analysis/standard/4k3/1P6/8/8/8/8/8/4K3_w_-_-_0_1)

The pawn moves to b8 and becomes a queen, with check. This is why pawns near the end of the board are so dangerous, and why stopping enemy pawns matters.

## Try it

1. White's king is on e1, rook on h1, nothing in between, neither has moved, but a black bishop attacks f1. Can White castle kingside?
2. A black pawn on c7 moves to c5, next to a white pawn on b5. What can White do on the next move?

**Answers:** 1. **No**: the king would pass through f1, an attacked square. 2. Capture **en passant**: bxc6, putting the white pawn on c6 and removing the black pawn from c5.
