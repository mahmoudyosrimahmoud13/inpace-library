# Flexible budgets and variances

At month end you compare actual results with the budget. But if you sold more than planned, of course costs are higher. A **flexible budget** fixes that: it shows what costs **should** have been at the **actual** volume.

## Nour's month: plan vs actual

Planned: 450 cakes at 120. Actual: **480 cakes** at an average price of **118** (some discounts).

Standard for ingredients: **10 units of mix per cake at 3.00 each** = 30 per cake. Actually used: **4,900 units** of mix at **3.20** each = 15,680.

## The flexible budget

| | Static budget (450) | Flexible budget (480) | Actual (480) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 54,000 | 57,600 | 56,640 |
| Ingredients (mix) | 13,500 | 14,400 | 15,680 |
| Other variable (20 per cake) | 9,000 | 9,600 | 9,600 |
| **Contribution margin** | **31,500** | **33,600** | **31,360** |
| Fixed costs | 21,000 | 21,000 | 21,000 |
| **Profit** | **10,500** | **12,600** | **10,360** |

Actual profit (10,360) is about the same as the plan (10,500), which hides two opposite stories. Variances show them.

## Sales variances

- **Sales volume variance** (static vs flexible, at budgeted CM): (480 − 450) × 70 = **2,100 favorable**. Selling 30 more cakes should have added 2,100.
- **Sales price variance:** (118 − 120) × 480 = **960 unfavorable**. Discounts cost 960.

## Ingredient variances

Flexible budget allows 480 × 10 = **4,800** units at 3.00 = 14,400. Actual 15,680. Total **1,280 unfavorable**, split into:

> **Price variance** = (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity = (3.20 − 3.00) × 4,900 = **980 U**
> **Quantity (usage) variance** = (Actual quantity − Standard quantity) × Standard price = (4,900 − 4,800) × 3.00 = **300 U**

Check: 980 + 300 = 1,280. ✓

## Reconciling

| | |
|---|---|
| Budgeted profit | 10,500 |
| Sales volume variance | +2,100 F |
| Sales price variance | −960 U |
| Ingredient price variance | −980 U |
| Ingredient quantity variance | −300 U |
| **Actual profit** | **10,360** |

## Reading variances

- The **purchaser** answers for the price variance: did the supplier raise prices, or did we buy in a rush?
- The **kitchen** answers for the quantity variance: waste, spoiled batches, a new baker?
- Variances are signals to **investigate**, not to blame. A favorable price variance from cheap, poor flour might cause an unfavorable quantity variance (more waste) and unhappy customers.
- Labor works the same way: a **rate** variance and an **efficiency** variance.

## Try it

Standard: 2 kg per unit at 5 per kg. Actual: 1,000 units made, 2,100 kg used, bought at 4.80 per kg. Find the price and quantity variances.

**Answer:** price = (4.80 − 5.00) × 2,100 = **420 F**; quantity = (2,100 − 2,000) × 5 = **500 U**; total 80 U.
