## The situation

Nour's bakery: price 120 a cake, variable cost 50, fixed costs 21,000 a month, currently selling **450 cakes** a month. Her oven can make at most **600 cakes** a month.

She has four decisions to make.

**A. A price change.** Her marketing friend says that cutting the price to 110 would raise sales to 540 cakes. Should she?

**B. A café chain's offer.** A chain wants **80 cakes a month at 75**, collected by them (variable cost 40 for these). Accept? (Check the oven limit with option A, too.)

**C. A third branch.** It would add 15,000 of fixed costs a month. Cakes there would have the same price and variable cost. How many cakes must the new branch sell to break even on its own? To add 6,000 of profit?

**D. A cash problem.** In April, Nour expects collections of 50,000 and payments of 58,000, with opening cash of 6,000 and a minimum balance of 5,000. How much must she borrow?

## Answers (don't look yet)

**A.** Now: 450 × 70 = 31,500 CM, profit 10,500. At 110: CM = 60, 540 × 60 = 32,400, profit **11,400**. Profit rises 900. Yes, if she believes the forecast, but the margin of safety shrinks: break-even rises to 21,000 ÷ 60 = 350 cakes. It's a small gain for more risk; testing it for a month first is wise.

**B.** Extra CM = 80 × (75 − 40) = **2,800** a month. Capacity: 450 + 80 = 530 ≤ 600, fine. But with option A (540 cakes) + 80 = 620 > 600: she'd lose 20 regular cakes (20 × 60 = 1,200 of CM), so the order adds 2,800 − 1,200 = 1,600. Still worth it, but less. Also ask: will regular customers hear about the 75 price?

**C.** Break-even = 15,000 ÷ 70 = **215 cakes** (214.3, rounded up). For 6,000 profit: (15,000 + 6,000) ÷ 70 = **300 cakes**.

**D.** 6,000 + 50,000 − 58,000 = −2,000. To end at 5,000 she must borrow **7,000** (or cut or delay payments).
