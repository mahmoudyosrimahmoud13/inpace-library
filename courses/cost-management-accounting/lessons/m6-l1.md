# Measuring performance

As a business grows, owners can't watch everything. They split it into units with a manager each, and measure each one on what it **controls**.

## Responsibility centers

| Center | Manager controls | Judged on | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost center** | Costs | Costs vs budget | The kitchen |
| **Revenue center** | Sales | Sales vs target | The catering sales team |
| **Profit center** | Costs and sales | Profit | A branch |
| **Investment center** | Costs, sales and investment | Return on investment | A whole division |

Rule: judge people only on what they can control. A branch manager shouldn't be blamed for head office rent allocated to her.

## Return on investment (ROI)

> **ROI = Operating profit ÷ Investment (assets used)**

Nour now has two branches:

| | Downtown | Suburb |
|---|---|---|
| Operating profit | 60,000 | 36,000 |
| Assets used | 400,000 | 200,000 |
| **ROI** | **15%** | **18%** |

Suburb earns less in total but uses its assets better.

ROI splits into **margin × turnover**: (profit ÷ sales) × (sales ÷ assets). It's the same idea as DuPont in the statements course.

## The problem with ROI, and residual income

Suppose Suburb's manager is offered a new project earning 16% on 100,000. Nour's required return (cost of capital) is **12%**, so the project is good for the business. But it would pull Suburb's ROI down from 18% toward 17%, so a manager judged on ROI might say no.

**Residual income** fixes this:

> **Residual income = Operating profit − (Required return × Investment)**

| | Downtown | Suburb | Suburb + project |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit | 60,000 | 36,000 | 52,000 |
| Required (12%) | 48,000 | 24,000 | 36,000 |
| **Residual income** | **12,000** | **12,000** | **16,000** |

Residual income rises with any project earning more than 12%, so managers' and owners' interests line up. **Economic value added (EVA)** is a refined version with adjustments.

## Beyond money: the balanced scorecard

Profit shows the past. A **balanced scorecard** adds measures that drive future profit, in four perspectives:

| Perspective | Question | Example measures for the bakery |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | How do we look to owners? | Profit, ROI, sales growth |
| **Customer** | How do customers see us? | Repeat customers, review rating, on-time deliveries |
| **Internal process** | What must we do well? | Waste %, orders made right first time, oven downtime |
| **Learning and growth** | Can we keep improving? | Staff training hours, new recipes launched, staff turnover |

The measures link: trained staff → less waste and fewer mistakes → happier customers → more repeat orders → more profit.

## Good KPIs

- Few (5 to 10 that matter), clear, owned by someone, measured regularly.
- A mix of **leading** indicators (training, on-time deliveries) and **lagging** ones (profit).
- Watch for gaming: measure "orders delivered on time", and someone may promise later delivery times.

## Try it

A division has profit 90,000 on assets 600,000. Required return 13%. Find ROI and residual income. Should it accept a project with profit 14,000 on 100,000 of assets?

**Answers:** ROI **15%**; RI = 90,000 − 78,000 = **12,000**. The project earns 14%, above 13%: RI rises by 1,000, so **accept** (even though ROI falls slightly, to 14.9%).
