# Read: convergence, momentum, Adam and normalization (7.3–7.4)

Read sections 7.3 and 7.4 to the end of the chapter (pages 218 to 232).

## Convergence (7.3)

Go back to the narrow valley. With plain gradient descent, a learning rate small enough not to bounce across the steep
direction is far too small to make progress along the shallow one. The book shows this with the Hessian's eigenvalues:
the learning rate is limited by the **largest** eigenvalue, but progress is limited by the **smallest**.

- **Momentum** (7.3.1): keep a running velocity. `Δw ← μΔw − η∇E`, then `w ← w + Δw`. Along the valley floor the
  gradients point the same way and add up; across the valley they flip sign and cancel. Typical μ is 0.9. **Nesterov
  momentum** looks one step ahead before computing the gradient.
- **Learning rate schedules** (7.3.2): start larger, then reduce the learning rate over training (linear, power,
  exponential decay). Large steps explore, small steps settle.
- **RMSProp and Adam** (7.3.3): give **each weight its own learning rate**, by dividing by a running average of its
  squared gradients. Weights with big, noisy gradients take smaller steps. **Adam** combines this with momentum, and adds
  a bias correction for the first few steps. It's the default optimizer for most deep learning today. Study the Adam
  algorithm box: you'll implement it on day 7.

## Normalization (7.4)

Badly scaled numbers make the valley narrow. Normalization makes it rounder.

- **Data normalization** (7.4.1): shift and scale each input to mean 0 and variance 1. Cheap, and it always helps.
- **Batch normalization** (7.4.2): do the same for the **hidden** units, using the statistics of the current mini-batch,
  then let the network re-scale with two learnable parameters per unit. At test time, use running averages of the
  statistics, since a test "batch" may be a single example.
- **Layer normalization** (7.4.3): normalize across the units of one layer for **each example** separately, instead of
  across the batch. No dependence on batch size, so it works the same in training and testing, and it's what
  transformers use (part 3).

## The point of this chapter

Training a deep network is an optimization problem with millions of variables, many minima and terrible scaling. Mini-
batches, good initialization, momentum or Adam, and normalization are what make it work in practice.
