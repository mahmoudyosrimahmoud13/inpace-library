# Read: limits of fixed basis functions, and multilayer networks (6.1–6.2)

Read the opening of chapter 6 and sections 6.1 and 6.2 (pages 171 to 186).

## Why fixed basis functions fail (6.1)

Part 1's models used basis functions chosen **before** seeing the data. Section 6.1 explains why that can't scale.

- **The curse of dimensionality** (6.1.1). Split each input axis into a few cells, and the number of cells grows
  exponentially with the number of inputs. The Iris example makes it concrete. Any method that needs to "cover" the
  input space with fixed bumps needs exponentially many of them.
- **High-dimensional spaces are strange** (6.1.2). Most of the volume of a high-dimensional ball lies in a thin shell
  near its surface, and most of a Gaussian's probability sits far from its peak. Your intuition from 2-D and 3-D
  pictures is unreliable here.
- **Data manifolds** (6.1.3). The rescue: real data doesn't fill its space. Images of handwritten digits are vectors with
  hundreds of numbers, but the digits only vary in a few ways (position, thickness, slant), so they lie near a
  low-dimensional surface, a **manifold**, inside that space.
- **Data-dependent basis functions** (6.1.4). So let the data decide where the basis functions go. The book mentions
  ideas like support vector machines, then gives the deep learning answer: make the basis functions themselves
  **learnable**.

## Multilayer networks (6.2)

A two-layer network is just that answer written down. The hidden units are basis functions with their own parameters:

```text
hidden:  zⱼ = h( Σᵢ wⱼᵢ⁽¹⁾ xᵢ + bⱼ⁽¹⁾ )          (learned basis functions)
output:  yₖ = f( Σⱼ wₖⱼ⁽²⁾ zⱼ + bₖ⁽²⁾ )          (a single-layer model on top of them)
```

- **Parameter matrices** (6.2.1): write the whole layer as one matrix product, `z = h(W⁽¹⁾x + b⁽¹⁾)`. That's how you'll
  code it.
- **Universal approximation** (6.2.2): a two-layer network with enough hidden units can approximate any reasonable
  function as closely as you like. It sounds like the end of the story, but read the caveat: it says nothing about how
  many units you need or how to find the weights.
- **Activation functions** (6.2.3): without a non-linear h, stacking layers collapses into one linear layer. Learn the
  shapes of tanh, the logistic sigmoid and **ReLU**, `max(0, a)`, the default in modern networks because its gradient
  doesn't shrink for positive inputs.
- **Weight-space symmetries** (6.2.4): swapping two hidden units, or flipping the signs around a tanh unit, gives the
  same function. So there are many equally good weight settings: the error surface has many equivalent minima.

## The idea to keep

A neural network is a single-layer model whose basis functions are learned. Everything else in the chapter is about how
deep to make it and what to put at its output.
