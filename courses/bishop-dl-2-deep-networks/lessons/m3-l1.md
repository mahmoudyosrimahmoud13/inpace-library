# Read: error surfaces and gradient descent (7.1–7.2)

Read the opening of chapter 7 and sections 7.1 and 7.2 (pages 209 to 218).

## Error surfaces (7.1)

Picture the error E(w) as a landscape over weight space. Training means walking downhill. Three facts shape everything:

- The surface has **many minima**. The weight-space symmetries from chapter 6 alone guarantee huge numbers of equivalent
  ones, and there are non-equivalent local minima too. We don't need the global minimum, just a good one.
- Near a minimum, the surface looks like a bowl: the **local quadratic approximation** (7.1.1) uses the gradient and the
  **Hessian** (the matrix of second derivatives). The Hessian's eigenvectors are the bowl's main directions, and its
  eigenvalues say how steep each direction is.
- When some directions are much steeper than others (a long, narrow valley), simple gradient descent struggles. Keep
  this picture in mind for the rest of the chapter.

## Gradient descent (7.2)

- **Why use gradients at all** (7.2.1): one gradient evaluation gives W pieces of information for about the cost of one
  error evaluation. That's why every practical method uses them.
- **Batch gradient descent** (7.2.2): step against the gradient of the error on the **whole** data set, `w ← w − η∇E`.
  Each step is expensive and, as the book says, it's a poor algorithm on its own.
- **Stochastic gradient descent** (7.2.3): use the gradient from **one** data point at a time. Much cheaper per step,
  naturally handles huge or streaming data sets, and the noise helps it escape bad stationary points.
- **Mini-batches** (7.2.4): the practical middle ground. Average the gradient over a small batch (tens to hundreds of
  examples). Shuffle the data each epoch. Note the book's point that a batch 100 times larger gives a gradient only about
  10 times more accurate, so bigger isn't always better.
- **Initialization** (7.2.5): you can't start at zero, because then every hidden unit computes the same thing and they
  stay identical forever: the weights must **break symmetry**. Use small random values with the right scale. For ReLU
  networks, **He initialization** draws each weight from a Gaussian with variance 2/M, where M is the number of inputs
  to the unit, so the signal neither explodes nor dies as it passes through many layers.
