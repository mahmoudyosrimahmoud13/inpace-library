## What is a data model?

A data model shows how data relates to the real world and to the business. Read why the authors say the big data era
skipped modeling (and made "data swamps"), and why the pendulum is swinging back. Then the three levels: **conceptual** (business entities
and how they relate), **logical** (columns, types, keys), **physical** (how it's stored in a particular database).

## Techniques for modeling batch analytical data

Four approaches. For each, write: who designed it, what the tables look like, and when you'd choose it.

- **Inmon**: a highly normalized central data warehouse (the single source of truth), feeding department **data
  marts**. Top-down.
- **Kimball**: **star schemas**, built close to how the business asks questions. Bottom-up. Read carefully: **fact
  tables** (at the **lowest grain** possible, numbers about events), **dimension tables** (the who, what, where,
  when), and **slowly changing dimensions** types 1, 2 and 3 (Tables 8-11 to 8-13). Note which type is the warehouse
  default and which is most used in practice. Also **conformed dimensions**.
- **Data Vault**: **hubs** (business keys), **links** (relationships) and **satellites** (attributes and history).
  Built for change and auditability; the business logic is applied later.
- **Wide denormalized tables**: one very wide table, made practical by columnar storage. Read the pros and cons.

Then *What If You Don't Model Your Data?*: the three questions to ask before skipping a model.

## What to take away

- Conceptual, logical and physical models go from business meaning to storage.
- Kimball's star schema (facts at the lowest grain, dimensions around them) is the most common analytics model.
- A type 2 slowly changing dimension keeps history, so facts stay tied to the truth at the time they happened.
