# Read: the Preface and Chapter 1, Data Engineering Described

Read the **Preface** quickly (it's short), then **Chapter 1** carefully. It sets up the vocabulary for the whole book.

## Preface

Notice what the authors say the book *isn't*: it isn't a tool tutorial. They teach the principles behind the tools,
so that what you learn still holds in ten years. That's also how this course works.

## Chapter 1: Data Engineering Described

**What is data engineering?** Read the definitions the authors collect from other people first, then theirs:
building and running the systems that take in raw data and produce **high-quality, consistent information** for the
people and systems downstream, such as analysts and machine learning. Ask yourself: where does the data engineer's
job start, and where does it stop?

**Evolution of the data engineer.** Four eras, from data warehousing in the 1980s and 1990s, through the web and
"big data" (Hadoop, MapReduce, the cloud), to today's focus on the **whole data lifecycle**. Look for the line
"what's old is new again": many "new" ideas are old ones with better tools.

**Data engineering and data science.** The data science hierarchy of needs (Figure 1-6): most of the work sits at
the bottom of the pyramid, collecting, moving, storing and cleaning data, and that's where data engineers work.

**Data maturity.** Three stages: *starting with data*, *scaling with data*, *leading with data*. For each stage, note
what a data engineer should focus on and the **mistakes to avoid**. This is one of the most useful parts of the book
for your first job: it tells you what "good work" means at the company you're in, not at Google.

**Skills.** Business skills (communication, scoping, cost control, learning) and technical skills. Note the four
primary languages the authors name, and their advice on keeping up: learn the fundamentals that don't change, and
watch new tools to see where things are going.

**Type A and Type B data engineers.** A for *abstraction* (assemble managed and off-the-shelf tools), B for *build*
(build custom systems where it gives the company an edge). Neither is better; it depends on the company.

**Inside an organization.** Internal-facing versus external-facing work, and the people upstream (software engineers,
data architects, DevOps and site reliability engineers) and downstream (analysts, data scientists, ML engineers).
Skim the job-title descriptions; keep the idea that you sit **between producers and consumers of data**.

## After reading

Pick a company you know (your employer, a client, or a shop you use a lot). Write down which maturity stage it's in,
and one thing a data engineer there should do first and one thing they should avoid.

## What to take away

- Data engineering turns raw data into reliable information that others use.
- What a good data engineer does depends on the company's data maturity.
- Data engineers sit between the people who produce data and the people who use it.
