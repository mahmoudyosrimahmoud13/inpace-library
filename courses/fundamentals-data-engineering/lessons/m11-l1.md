# Read: Chapter 9, Serving Data for Analytics, Machine Learning, and Reverse ETL

Serving is where data finally becomes useful to someone. It's also where a data team earns or loses **trust**: one
wrong number on a dashboard can undo months of good work. Read the whole chapter.

## General considerations for serving data

- **Trust**: read the opening carefully. Data validation and observability, and service level agreements (SLAs) and
  objectives (SLOs) with data users.
- **What's the use case, and who's the user?** Start from the action someone will take with the data.
- **Data products**: D. J. Patil's definition, and the questions to ask before building one.
- **Self-service or not?** When self-service analytics works, and why it often fails.
- **Data definitions and logic**: why "customer" or "active user" must mean one thing everywhere.
- **Data mesh**, from the serving side.

## Analytics

**Business analytics** (dashboards, reports, ad hoc analysis), **operational analytics** (live data to act on now),
and **embedded analytics** (analytics shown to your customers inside your product). Note how latency and reliability
needs grow from the first to the last.

## Machine learning

Read *What a Data Engineer Should Know About ML*: the authors' list (supervised versus unsupervised learning,
classification versus regression, classic models versus deep learning, encoding categories and embeddings, batch
versus online learning, feature stores) is a good map of where data engineering ends and ML engineering begins.

## Ways to serve data for analytics and ML

File exchange, databases, streaming systems, **query federation**, **data sharing**, **semantic and metrics layers**,
and serving data in notebooks. For each, ask: who reads it, how fresh must it be, and who owns the definitions?

## Reverse ETL

Serving data back into the tools where people work: the CRM, the ad platform, the email tool. Read the warning about
**feedback loops**: what happens when a model's output feeds the system that produces its own input.

## Whom you'll work with, and the undercurrents

Security (who can see what in a dashboard), data management, DataOps (monitoring data health), architecture,
orchestration, software engineering.

## After reading

Pick a dashboard you use. Who is it for, what decision does it support, and how would you know if one of its numbers
was wrong?

## What to take away

- Serving is about trust: validated data, clear definitions, agreed freshness.
- Start from the user and the decision, not the tool.
- Define each business metric once; serve it everywhere from that one definition.
