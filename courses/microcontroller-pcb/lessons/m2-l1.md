# The minimal circuit

A microcontroller needs surprisingly little to run: power, a clock, a reset pull-up and decoupling. Here is the complete minimal circuit, with one LED to prove it works:

![ATmega328P minimal circuit: +5 V with 100 nF, 10 kΩ reset pull-up and button, 16 MHz crystal with two 22 pF capacitors, an LED on pin 19](mcu-minimal.png)

**How to read it:** the box is the chip, and the small numbers outside it are pin numbers. Power comes in at the top: +5 V to VCC (7) and AVCC (20), with a 100 nF capacitor to ground right next to them. On the left: the reset pin (1) is pulled up to +5 V by 10 kΩ, and the reset button can pull it to ground. Below that, the 16 MHz crystal sits between XTAL1 (9) and XTAL2 (10), each side with a 22 pF capacitor to ground. Pin 8 (and 22) go to ground. On the right, pin 19 (Arduino D13) drives an LED through 330 Ω, and pins 2 and 3 go out to a USB-serial adapter.

## 1. Power and decoupling

- +5 V to **VCC (7)** and **AVCC (20)**; ground to **GND (8 and 22)**.
- A **100 nF** ceramic capacitor between VCC and GND, and another between AVCC and GND, each **as close to the pins as possible**.
- A larger **10 µF** capacitor where power enters the board.

Missing decoupling is the classic cause of boards that "work sometimes".

## 2. The clock

The processor needs a steady clock. Options:

| Clock | Accuracy | Parts | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **16 MHz crystal** + 2 × 22 pF | very good | 3 | the Arduino standard; needed for reliable serial at high speeds |
| 16 MHz ceramic resonator | good | 1 (3-pin) | cheaper, smaller, slightly less accurate |
| Internal 8 MHz oscillator | about ±1 to 10% | 0 | fine for many projects; set by "fuses" |

The crystal's two capacitors value comes from the crystal's datasheet ("load capacitance"); 22 pF is right for most 16 MHz crystals. Place the crystal and its capacitors **very close** to pins 9 and 10, with short tracks.

## 3. Reset

The RESET pin restarts the chip when pulled low. Keep it high with a **10 kΩ pull-up** to +5 V. Add a **push button** to ground for manual reset. (For automatic reset when uploading from a computer, a 100 nF capacitor from the USB-serial chip's DTR line goes to RESET: module 3.)

## 4. One LED to prove it's alive

An LED with 330 Ω on PB5 (pin 19, Arduino D13) is the universal "it works" test: the first program blinks it.

```python
v, v_led, r = 5.0, 2.0, 330
i = (v - v_led) / r
print(f"LED current {i * 1000:.1f} mA: well under the 20 mA per pin limit")
```

```output
LED current 9.1 mA: well under the 20 mA per pin limit
```

## Try it on a breadboard first

Build exactly this on a breadboard with a DIP ATmega328P (buy one "with Arduino bootloader" to make the next module easier), 5 V from a USB breadboard power module, and the parts above. Prototype first, then design the PCB. It's the same rule as in the PCB course, and it matters even more with microcontrollers.

## Summary

- Minimal ATmega328P: 5 V to VCC and AVCC, both GNDs, 100 nF at each power pin, 10 µF at the input.
- Clock: 16 MHz crystal with two 22 pF capacitors close to pins 9 and 10 (or a resonator, or the internal 8 MHz).
- RESET with a 10 kΩ pull-up and a button; an LED on D13 to test.
