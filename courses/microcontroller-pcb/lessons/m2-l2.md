# Powering the board

Your board needs a clean, safe 5 V. The easiest source is **USB**: every computer and phone charger provides 5 V.

## USB-C power input

![USB-C VBUS through a 500 mA polyfuse to +5 V, with 10 µF and 100 nF capacitors and a power LED](mcu-usb-power.png)

**How to read it:** USB-C's power pin (**VBUS**) goes through a resettable fuse (the rectangle) to the board's +5 V line (red). Two capacitors from +5 V to ground (blue) smooth the supply: a 10 µF for slower dips and a 100 nF for fast ones. On the right, a 1 kΩ resistor and an LED show that power is on.

The parts:

- **Polyfuse** (PTC, for example 500 mA): if something shorts, it heats up and limits the current; it resets itself when cooled. It protects both your board and the computer's USB port.
- **10 µF + 100 nF**: bulk and fast decoupling where power enters.
- **Power LED** with 1 kΩ: about 3 mA, just enough to see.
- **CC1 and CC2 resistors** (5.1 kΩ each, to ground): a USB-C charger only turns on 5 V when it sees them. Forgetting them is the most common USB-C mistake: the board works from an old USB-A cable but not from a USB-C charger.

Using a USB-C connector in KiCad: choose a "USB-C receptacle, power only" (USB 2.0 type) footprint that your factory can assemble, or a through-hole micro-USB connector, which is easier to hand-solder.

## Reverse polarity protection

If your board can also be powered from a battery or DC jack, someone will eventually connect it backwards. Protect it:

| Method | Cost | Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Series **Schottky diode** (e.g. SS14) | 1 part | about 0.3 to 0.4 V |
| **P-channel MOSFET** "ideal diode" | 1 MOSFET + 1 resistor | almost none |

USB connectors can't be plugged in backwards electrically, so a USB-only board can skip this.

## Battery power and 3.3 V

| Source | What to add |
|---|---|
| 9 V battery or 7 to 12 V DC jack | a 5 V regulator (7805 or a buck module) |
| 3 × AA (4.5 V) | runs the ATmega directly at 8 MHz (internal clock); 16 MHz is out of spec below about 4.5 V |
| A Li-ion cell (3.7 V) | run at 3.3 V and 8 MHz, with an LDO such as MCP1700-3.3 |
| 3.3 V sensors on a 5 V board | an LDO (AMS1117-3.3) for the sensor, and level shifting for its inputs |

```python
def ldo_heat(vin, vout, amps):
    return (vin - vout) * amps

print(f"AMS1117-3.3 from 5 V at 100 mA: {ldo_heat(5, 3.3, 0.1):.2f} W (fine)")
print(f"7805 from 12 V at 300 mA: {ldo_heat(12, 5, 0.3):.2f} W (needs a heatsink: use a buck module)")
```

```output
AMS1117-3.3 from 5 V at 100 mA: 0.17 W (fine)
7805 from 12 V at 300 mA: 2.10 W (needs a heatsink: use a buck module)
```

## Power budget

Add up what the board draws, and keep within the source's limit (a computer USB 2.0 port guarantees 500 mA):

```python
budget = {"ATmega328P at 16 MHz": 15, "power LED": 3, "status LED": 9, "USB-serial chip": 10, "sensor": 2}
total = sum(budget.values())
print(f"total {total} mA of 500 mA available -> {total / 500:.0%} used")
```

```output
total 39 mA of 500 mA available -> 8% used
```

Plenty of headroom: you could add a small servo motor, but not a big one (those need their own supply).

## Summary

- USB-C: VBUS through a polyfuse, 10 µF + 100 nF, a power LED, and 5.1 kΩ on CC1 and CC2.
- Protect battery or DC-jack inputs against reverse polarity (Schottky diode or P-MOSFET).
- Lower voltages need lower clock speeds; check regulator heat and add up a power budget.
