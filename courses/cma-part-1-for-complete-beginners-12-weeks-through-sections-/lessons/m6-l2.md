## 1. Learning curves

Each time cumulative output **doubles**, cumulative average time per unit falls by a fixed percentage.

| Cumulative units | Average hours (80% curve) | Total hours |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| 2 | 80.0 | 160.0 |
| 4 | 64.0 | 256.0 |
| 8 | 51.2 | 409.6 |

**Trap:** hours for units 5-8 = 409.6 − 256 = 153.6, which is 38.4 each — not 51.2. Learning curves
matter for new, labour-intensive work: pricing contracts, setting labour standards and planning
capacity.

## 2. Expected value

| 🎲 Scenario | Probability | Profit $ | Weighted $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | 0.30 | 200,000 | 60,000 |
| Normal | 0.50 | 80,000 | 40,000 |
| Weak | 0.20 | (40,000) | (8,000) |
| **Expected value** | 1.00 | | **92,000** |

The expected value is a long-run average, not a forecast for one year. A risk-averse manager may
deliberately choose a smaller, safer outcome.

## 3. Time series

| 🧩 Component | 🧠 Meaning | 💡 Example |
|---|---|---|
| Trend | Long-term direction | Ice-cream sales up 3% a year |
| Seasonal | Repeats within the year | July peak |
| Cyclical | Multi-year swings | Boom and recession |
| Random | Unpredictable noise | A heatwave |

| 🧮 Technique | 🧠 How | 👍 / 👎 |
|---|---|---|
| Moving average | Average of the last n periods | Smooth, but lags turning points |
| Weighted moving average | Recent periods weigh more | More responsive, still lags |
| Exponential smoothing | New forecast = old forecast + α (actual − old forecast) | Flexible; needs a good α |
| Regression trend | Line fitted against time | Good for steady trends |

**Worked example (α = 0.3):** last forecast 1,000, actual 1,200 → new forecast = 1,000 + 0.3 × 200 =
**1,060**. High α reacts fast and jumps; low α is smooth and slow.

**Measuring error:** MAD (average absolute miss), MSE (punishes big misses), MAPE (miss as a % of
actual) and RSFE (a large positive running total means you keep forecasting too low).
