# Pins and skewers: attacks along a line

Rooks, bishops and queens attack along lines. When two enemy pieces stand on the same line, you can attack through one to the other.

## The pin

A **pin** attacks a piece that can't move (or shouldn't) because a **more valuable piece** stands behind it.

- **Absolute pin:** the piece behind is the **king**. The pinned piece is not allowed to move at all.
- **Relative pin:** the piece behind is something else valuable, like the queen. The pinned piece *may* move, but you lose what's behind it.

![Chess board](board-742546db78.png)

*Black to move (the last move is shown in yellow).* [Open on Lichess to play it out](https://lichess.org/analysis/standard/r1bqkbnr/ppp2ppp/2np4/1B2p3/3PP3/5N2/PPP2PPP/RNBQK2R_b_KQkq_-_2_4)

The bishop on b5 pins Black's knight on c6 to the king on e8: the d7 square is empty, so the knight can't legally move. Pinned pieces are **bad defenders** (the knight no longer really guards e5 or d4) and **good targets**: attack a pinned piece again, for example with a pawn, and it often can't escape.

A very common relative pin: a white bishop on **g5** pinning a black knight on **f6** to the queen on **d8**.

**Breaking a pin:** move the piece behind out of the line, put another piece in between, or attack the pinning piece (...a6 asking the bishop on b5 to decide).

## The skewer

A **skewer** is a pin in reverse: you attack a **valuable** piece, it moves out of the way, and you take the piece **behind** it.

![Chess board](board-a4b952c08e.png)

*White to move.* [Open on Lichess to play it out](https://lichess.org/analysis/standard/4q3/8/8/8/4k3/8/8/R5K1_w_-_-_0_1)

**Re1+ (rook to e1, check)** checks the king on e4. The king must step off the e-file, and the rook takes the queen on e8. Skewers with check are the strongest kind.

## How to find them

Look for two enemy pieces on the **same line** (file, rank or diagonal) with nothing between them, or with only something you can remove. Then ask: which of my long-range pieces can get onto that line?
