# RAG in plain words

A language model knows what was in its training text, up to a date, and nothing about your documents. Ask it about
your company's API or last week's policy and it guesses, fluently.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** fixes this in two steps:

1. **Retrieve.** Search your documents for the few pieces most likely to answer the question.
2. **Generate.** Put those pieces in the prompt and ask the model to answer *from them*, and to say which ones it used.

So a RAG system is mostly a search engine with a model at the end. That's the most useful thing to remember from this
course: **if the right text never reaches the prompt, no model can answer correctly from it.** Most RAG problems are
search problems.

## The parts

- **Documents** are split into **chunks**: pieces small enough to fit several in a prompt.
- An **index** makes chunks fast to find: an inverted index (word → chunks) for keyword search, or a matrix of
  **embeddings** (vectors that represent meaning) for semantic search.
- A **retriever** takes a question and returns the top k chunks.
- A **prompt** puts the chunks, numbered, next to the question, with rules: use only the sources, cite them, say "I
  don't know" when they don't cover it.
- The **model** writes the answer.

## Measure from day one

You can't improve what you don't measure, and RAG systems look fine on the three questions you try by hand. So the
first thing you build is a **test set**: questions with known right documents. Two numbers tell you how search is
doing:

- **recall@k**: the share of questions where a right document is somewhere in the top k results. If it's not in the
  top k, it won't be in the prompt.
- **MRR** (mean reciprocal rank): for each question, 1 / the position of the first right result (1 for first place,
  0.5 for second, 0 if missing), averaged. It rewards putting the right document first.

## BM25 in one paragraph

BM25 is the keyword ranking behind most search engines, and still a strong baseline. A document scores points for
each question word it contains. **Rare words count more**: "rmtree" says far more than "file" (that's the idf part). A
word that appears five times counts more than once, but **not five times more** (k1 limits it). And long documents
are **marked down a little**, because they contain many words just by being long (b). That's all of it, about 25
lines of Python in project 1.
