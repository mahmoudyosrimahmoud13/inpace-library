## Transformation of densities (2.4)

If x has density p(x) and you compute y = g(x), the density of y is **not** just p(g⁻¹(y)). You also have to account
for how the function stretches or squeezes space:

```text
p_y(y) = p_x(x) · |dx/dy|
```

In many dimensions, `|dx/dy|` becomes the absolute **determinant of the Jacobian** matrix. Why care now? Two reasons.

- One odd consequence the book points out: the **most probable value** (the peak) of a density moves when you change
  variables, in a way an ordinary function's maximum doesn't. Keep that in mind whenever someone reports "the most
  likely value".
- Much later, **normalizing flows** (chapter 18) are built entirely on this formula: a network transforms a simple
  Gaussian into a complicated distribution, and the Jacobian tells you the new density.

You can skim 2.4.1 the first time, but don't skip it entirely.

## Information theory (2.5)

Read this part carefully. Cross-entropy and KL divergence are everywhere in deep learning.

**Entropy (2.5.1).** The information you get from seeing an event is `h(x) = −log p(x)`: rare events surprise you more.
The **entropy** is the average surprise, `H[x] = −Σ p(x) log p(x)`. The book's example with 8 equally likely states
gives 3 bits; making the states unequal lowers it to 2 bits, and that number is also the shortest average code length
you can use to send the state. With natural logarithms the unit is the **nat** instead of the bit.

**Physics view (2.5.2).** An optional detour that explains where the word comes from. Skim it.

**Differential entropy and maximum entropy (2.5.3–2.5.4).** For continuous variables, the same idea with an integral.
The important result: among all distributions with a given variance, the **Gaussian has the largest entropy**. It's
the "least committed" choice, one more reason it shows up everywhere.

**KL divergence (2.5.5).** The **Kullback–Leibler divergence** `KL(p‖q) = −∫ p(x) ln( q(x) / p(x) ) dx` measures how much
extra information you need when you use a model q to describe data that really comes from p. Remember three facts:

- `KL(p‖q) ≥ 0`, and it's zero only when p = q.
- It's **not symmetric**: `KL(p‖q)` and `KL(q‖p)` are usually different, so it isn't a true distance.
- **Minimizing KL(p‖q) over the model q is the same as maximizing the likelihood** of data drawn from p. This is why
  training a classifier with cross-entropy and fitting it by maximum likelihood are the same thing.

**Conditional entropy and mutual information (2.5.6–2.5.7).** Mutual information `I[x, y]` is how much knowing y tells
you about x. It's zero exactly when x and y are independent. You'll meet it again with latent variables.

## The one sentence to keep

Entropy measures uncertainty, KL measures the mismatch between two distributions, and fitting a model by maximum
likelihood means making that mismatch as small as possible.
