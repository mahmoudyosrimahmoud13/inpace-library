# Read: periodic variables, the exponential family, and nonparametric methods (3.3–3.5)

Read sections 3.3 to 3.5 (pages 89 to 110). The first two parts can be read lightly; the third is worth more care.

## Periodic variables (3.3): skim

Angles and times of day wrap around: 359° is close to 1°, but their average isn't 180°. The **von Mises**
distribution is the Gaussian's cousin on a circle. Read it to know it exists; you won't need its details later.

## The exponential family (3.4): read for the big picture

Bernoulli, multinomial, Gaussian and many others share one form:

```text
p(x | η) = h(x) g(η) exp( ηᵀ u(x) )
```

Three things to take away:

- The **natural parameters** η: for the Bernoulli, η is the log-odds `ln(μ / (1 − μ))`, and turning it back into μ gives
  the **logistic sigmoid**. For the multinomial, it gives the **softmax**. That's where the activation functions of
  chapter 5 come from.
- **Sufficient statistics** (3.4.1): for any member of the family, maximum likelihood only needs the sum of u(x) over the
  data, not the data itself.
- This family is what makes the "canonical link" result at the end of chapter 5 work.

## Nonparametric methods (3.5): read carefully

So far, every model had a fixed number of parameters. Nonparametric methods let the data decide the shape.

- **Histograms** (3.5.1): split the line into bins and count. Easy, but the result depends on the bin width, and in D
  dimensions you need about M^D bins. With 10 bins per axis and 20 dimensions that's 10²⁰ bins: an early taste of the
  **curse of dimensionality**.
- **Kernel density estimation** (3.5.2): put a small bump (a kernel, often a Gaussian) on every data point and add them
  up. The bump width h works like a smoothing knob: too small and the estimate is spiky, too large and real structure
  is washed out.
- **Nearest neighbours** (3.5.3): fix how many neighbours K you want, and let the region grow until it contains K
  points. The same idea gives the K-nearest-neighbour **classifier**: label a new point by a vote of its K nearest
  training points.

The book's point: these methods need to keep **all** the training data, and they struggle in high dimensions. Neural
networks are a way to get flexible models that stay compact. Keep the smoothing-parameter picture in mind, because
choosing h or K is the same over-fitting trade-off as choosing M in chapter 1.
