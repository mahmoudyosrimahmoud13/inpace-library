## Back to the bent coin

Section 2.6 returns to the coin from the start of the chapter. You don't know which side is heads, so you start with a
**prior**, flip the coin, and update with Bayes' theorem to get a **posterior**. The posterior after some flips becomes
the prior for the next flips: learning as a running update.

The book also shows the weakness of pure maximum likelihood. Toss a fair-looking coin three times and get three heads:
maximum likelihood says the probability of heads is 1, so tails is impossible. Any sensible prior gives a less extreme
answer. With little data, the prior matters a lot; with lots of data, the data wins.

## Parameters as random variables (2.6.1)

The big step: treat the **weights w** of a model as uncertain too, with a prior p(w) and, after seeing data D, a
posterior:

```text
posterior ∝ likelihood × prior          p(w | D) ∝ p(D | w) p(w)
```

Read the comparison of the **frequentist** view (w is a fixed unknown number; the data set is what's random) and the
**Bayesian** view (there's one data set; uncertainty about w is described by a distribution). You don't have to pick a
side, but you should be able to state each one.

## Regularization explained (2.6.2)

This is the payoff of the chapter. Instead of maximizing the likelihood, maximize the posterior. This is the **MAP**
(maximum a posteriori) estimate. Take the negative log, and choose a Gaussian prior on the weights centred on zero:
the prior adds a term proportional to `‖w‖²` to the error. That is **exactly** the regularizer `(λ/2)‖w‖²` from
chapter 1, and λ turns out to be the noise variance divided by the prior variance: the noisier the data, or the more firmly you believe the weights are small, the stronger the penalty.

So the chain from chapter 1 is now complete:

| Chapter 1 | Probability view |
|---|---|
| Sum-of-squares error | Gaussian noise on the targets (maximum likelihood) |
| Adding `(λ/2)‖w‖²` | A Gaussian prior on the weights (MAP) |
| Choosing λ | Choosing how strongly you believe weights are small |

## Bayesian machine learning (2.6.3)

A fully Bayesian model doesn't keep just one best w. It averages predictions over *all* w, weighted by the posterior.
That gives honest uncertainty, and it automatically avoids over-fitting. The catch: for a deep network with millions
of weights, that average is far too expensive to compute exactly. That's why most of deep learning uses MAP-style
training with regularization, while keeping the Bayesian picture in mind to explain why it works.
