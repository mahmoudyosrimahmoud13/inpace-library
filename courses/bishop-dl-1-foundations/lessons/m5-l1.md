# Read: linear regression (4.1)

Read the opening of chapter 4 and section 4.1 (pages 111 to 120). It turns chapter 1's curve fitting into a general
model, and the result is your first neural network: a network with a single layer.

## Basis functions (4.1.1)

Replace the powers of x with any fixed functions φⱼ(x), called **basis functions**:

```text
y(x, w) = Σⱼ wⱼ φⱼ(x) = wᵀ φ(x)        (with φ₀(x) = 1 for the bias)
```

The model is **non-linear in x** but still **linear in w**, which is what makes it easy to solve. Figure 4.2 shows three
choices: polynomials (global: changing one weight moves the whole curve), **Gaussian bumps** (local: each one only
matters near its centre μⱼ), and **sigmoids**. Look at Figure 4.1 too: drawn as a diagram of nodes and links, this model
*is* a single-layer network.

The book's key point is in the paragraph about feature extraction: for decades people hand-designed the φⱼ. Deep
learning's big idea is to **learn** them from data instead. Chapter 6 does exactly that.

## Likelihood and least squares (4.1.2–4.1.4)

Assume Gaussian noise on the targets, as in chapter 2. Maximizing the likelihood gives the **normal equations** and
their solution:

```text
w_ML = (ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹ Φᵀ t
```

where Φ is the **design matrix**: one row per data point, one column per basis function. `(ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹Φᵀ` is the
**Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse**. Two details worth noticing:

- The bias w₀ just makes up the difference between the average target and the average of the basis functions.
- The maximum likelihood noise variance is the leftover (residual) spread of the targets around the fitted curve.

**Geometry (4.1.4).** Picture the N targets as one vector t in N-dimensional space. The columns of Φ span a smaller
subspace. Least squares finds the point in that subspace closest to t: an **orthogonal projection**. If you took
*Maths for ML 1*, this is the projection from its chapter 3.

## Sequential learning (4.1.5)

When data arrives one point at a time, or the data set is too big to process at once, update the weights after each
point:

```text
w ← w + η (tₙ − wᵀφₙ) φₙ
```

This is **stochastic gradient descent** on the squared error, known here as the LMS algorithm. Read it carefully: it is
the ancestor of how every deep network is trained.

## Regularization and multiple outputs (4.1.6–4.1.7)

Adding `(λ/2)‖w‖²` changes the solution to `w = (λI + ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹ Φᵀ t`. This is called **weight decay**; statisticians
call it a **shrinkage** method, and it also keeps the matrix invertible. (Chapter 9 adds other penalties, such as the
lasso, which pushes some weights to exactly zero.) For several outputs, each output gets its own weights, and the
solution separates into independent problems that share the same pseudo-inverse.
