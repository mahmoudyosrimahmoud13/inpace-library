# Read: the deep learning revolution (chapter 1)

Read the whole of chapter 1 (pages 1 to 22). It's the gentlest chapter in the book and it sets up almost everything
that follows.

## What to look for

**1.1, the impact of deep learning.** Four short stories: medical diagnosis, protein structure, image synthesis and
large language models. Don't try to understand how any of them work yet. Notice what they have in common: in every
case, nobody wrote the rules by hand. A model with many adjustable numbers (**parameters**) was shown lots of
examples, and the numbers were tuned until the model did the job. That's the whole idea of machine learning.

**1.2, a tutorial example.** This is the most important part of the chapter. Slow down here. The book builds a tiny
problem that comes back again and again:

- The true curve is `sin(2πx)`. We get only **10 noisy points** from it, spread evenly between 0 and 1.
- We fit a **polynomial** of order M: `y(x, w) = w0 + w1·x + w2·x² + … + wM·x^M`.
- We choose the weights w by making the **sum-of-squares error** as small as possible: the squared distances
  between the curve and the points, added up.

Then the book asks the big question: which order M should we pick? Look closely at the pictures for M = 0, 1, 3 and 9.

- M = 0 and M = 1 are too simple. They miss the shape. This is **under-fitting**.
- M = 3 looks about right.
- M = 9 passes through every single point, so its training error is **zero**, and yet it swings wildly between the
  points. It has learned the noise, not the curve. This is **over-fitting**.

The key lesson: **a small error on the training data doesn't mean a good model.** What we care about is
**generalization**: how well the model predicts points it has never seen. That's why the book measures error on a
separate **test set** of 100 new points, and plots training and test error side by side as M grows.

Two cures for over-fitting appear in this section, and both come back for the rest of the book:

1. **More data.** With 100 points instead of 10, even the M = 9 polynomial behaves.
2. **Regularization.** Add a penalty `(λ/2)·‖w‖²` to the error, so huge weights cost something. With ln λ = −18 the
   wild swings disappear; with λ too large (ln λ = 0) the curve is flattened too much.

Notice also **model selection**: you can't choose M or λ using the training error, because it always prefers the most
complex model. You set aside **validation data** (or use **cross-validation**) to choose them.

**1.3, a brief history.** Read it like a story. Remember three milestones: the single-layer **perceptron** and its
limits, **backpropagation** (which made training many layers possible), and **deep networks** with huge data and
GPUs, which made it work in practice. Notice the idea of **representation learning**: the hidden layers learn useful
features by themselves, instead of a person designing them.

## Words to know

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Parameters (weights) | The adjustable numbers inside a model, here w0 … wM |
| Training set | The examples the model learns from |
| Test set | Fresh examples kept aside to measure generalization |
| Over-fitting | Fitting the noise of the training data, so new predictions get worse |
| Regularization | A penalty that discourages extreme parameter values |
| Hyperparameter | A setting you choose outside of training, like M or λ |
