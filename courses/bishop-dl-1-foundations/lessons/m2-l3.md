## Densities (2.2)

For a continuous variable like a height or a temperature, the probability of any *exact* value is zero. Instead we use
a **probability density** p(x): the probability of landing in a small interval is about `p(x) · δx`, and probabilities
of ranges are areas under the curve. Two things that surprise people:

- A density can be bigger than 1. Only its **area** must be 1.
- The sum and product rules still hold, with integrals in place of sums.

The **expectation** `E[f] = ∫ p(x) f(x) dx` is the average value of f when x is drawn from p. With N samples, you can
approximate it by the plain average `(1/N) Σ f(xₙ)`. That simple fact, averaging over samples, is how every neural
network in this book is trained. The **variance** measures spread around the mean, and the **covariance** measures how
two variables move together.

## The Gaussian (2.3)

The Gaussian (normal) distribution has two parameters, the mean μ and the variance σ²:

```text
N(x | μ, σ²) = 1 / sqrt(2πσ²) · exp( −(x − μ)² / (2σ²) )
```

You will meet it on almost every page of the book, so get comfortable with its shape: a bell centred on μ, with σ
setting its width.

**Likelihood (2.3.2).** Suppose you have N data points and want to choose μ and σ². The **likelihood** is the
probability of the observed data seen as a function of the parameters. Because the points are independent, it's a
product, and it's easier to work with its logarithm, which turns the product into a sum. Maximizing it gives two
results you should be able to derive yourself:

- the best μ is the **sample mean**,
- the best σ² is the **average squared distance** from that mean.

**The bias of maximum likelihood (2.3.3).** That variance estimate is systematically too small, by a factor
`(N − 1)/N`: it measures spread around the sample mean, which sits closer to the data than the true mean does. With
large N it barely matters, but it's the first example of a theme that runs through the book: **maximum likelihood
over-fits**, especially with little data.

**Linear regression as likelihood (2.3.4).** This is the key idea of the chapter, so read it twice. Go back to the
curve fitting of chapter 1, and assume each target is the curve plus Gaussian noise: `t = y(x, w) + noise`. Then
maximizing the likelihood of the targets is **exactly the same** as minimizing the sum-of-squares error. The error
function from chapter 1 wasn't arbitrary: it's what Gaussian noise implies. And now we also get an estimate of the
noise level, so the model can say how uncertain its predictions are.
