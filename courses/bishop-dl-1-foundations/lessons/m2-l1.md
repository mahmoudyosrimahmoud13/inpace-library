## Why a deep learning book starts with probability

Chapter 1 fitted a curve by minimizing an error. That works, but it leaves questions open: how sure is the model? Why
squared error and not something else? How do we include what we knew before seeing the data? Probability answers all
three, and the rest of the book is written in its language. The opening pages make one point worth keeping: there are
two kinds of uncertainty.

- **Noise in the data** (the book's "aleatoric" uncertainty): even with infinite data, a coin flip stays random.
- **Not enough data** ("epistemic" uncertainty): it shrinks as you collect more examples.

The bent-coin example shows that probability can describe both **frequencies** of repeatable events and plain
**uncertainty** about something you don't know (which side of the coin is heads). The second view is the
**Bayesian** one, and it's the one the book uses.

## The two rules (2.1.2)

Everything in probability follows from two rules. The book derives them by counting points in a grid of cells
(Figure 2.4). Make sure you can redo that counting argument.

- **Sum rule**: `p(X) = Σ_Y p(X, Y)`. To get the probability of X alone, add up the joint probability over every value
  of Y. This is called **marginalizing** Y out.
- **Product rule**: `p(X, Y) = p(Y | X) p(X)`. The chance of both equals the chance of X times the chance of Y given X.

From these two, in one line, comes **Bayes' theorem** (2.1.3):

## The screening example (2.1.1 and 2.1.4)

This is the part to really understand. The numbers: 1% of people have cancer, the test wrongly says "positive" for 3%
of healthy people, and it misses 10% of people who do have cancer. Someone tests positive. How likely is it that they
have cancer?

Most people guess around 90%. The book's answer is about **23%**. Work it through yourself before reading the book's
solution:

- Out of 1000 people, 10 have cancer, and the test catches 9 of them.
- 990 are healthy, and 3% of them, about 30, also test positive.
- So of roughly 39 positives, only 9 are real: 9 / 39 ≈ 23%.

The disease is rare, so false positives from the huge healthy group swamp the true positives. This is the **prior**
at work: before the test, the chance was 1% (**prior probability**). After a positive test, it's 23% (**posterior
probability**). The test moved the belief a lot, but not to certainty.

## Independence (2.1.6)

X and Y are **independent** when `p(X, Y) = p(X) p(Y)`, which is the same as saying `p(Y | X) = p(Y)`: knowing X tells
you nothing about Y. Most of the models in this book are, at heart, careful statements about which variables depend on
which.
