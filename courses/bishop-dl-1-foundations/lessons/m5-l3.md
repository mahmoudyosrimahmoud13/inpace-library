## Decision theory for regression (4.2)

A model gives you a whole predictive distribution `p(t | x)`, but sometimes you need one number. Which one? It depends
on how you're penalized for being wrong (the **loss**).

- With **squared loss**, the best prediction is the **conditional mean** `E[t | x]`, called the regression function.
- With absolute loss it's the conditional **median** (the book's Minkowski loss with q = 1).

The expected squared loss splits into two parts: how far your prediction is from the conditional mean (you can reduce
this) plus the natural noise of the targets around that mean (you can't reduce this, no matter how good the model).
Separate **inference** (learning `p(t | x)`) from **decision** (picking a value): you can change the loss later without
retraining.

## The bias–variance trade-off (4.3)

This is a classic. Imagine training the same model on many different data sets of the same size. Then the expected
error on new points splits into three parts:

```text
expected loss = (bias)² + variance + noise
```

- **Bias**: how far the *average* model (averaged over data sets) is from the truth. Simple, rigid models have high bias.
- **Variance**: how much the model changes from one data set to another. Flexible models have high variance.
- **Noise**: the irreducible part from 4.2.

The book's experiment (Figure 4.7): 100 data sets of 25 points each from `sin(2πx)`, each fitted with 24 Gaussian basis
functions and weight decay. With large λ, every fit is smooth and similar (low variance) but all of them miss the sine
(high bias). With small λ, the fits wiggle and differ a lot (high variance), but their average is close to the sine
(low bias). The best λ balances the two.

Read the book's caution at the end of the section: in practice you only have **one** data set, so you can't compute
bias and variance directly. The decomposition is a way of *thinking* about over-fitting, not a recipe. And as you'll see
in chapter 9 (double descent), very large networks sometimes break this simple picture.
