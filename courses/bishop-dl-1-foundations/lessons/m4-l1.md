# Read: discrete variables and the multivariate Gaussian (3.1–3.2)

Read sections 3.1 and 3.2 (pages 65 to 89). This is the most maths-heavy reading of the course. Plan two sittings, and
don't worry about following every line of algebra in 3.2.4 to 3.2.6 the first time: get the results and the pictures.

## Discrete variables (3.1)

- **Bernoulli** (3.1.1): one yes/no variable with `p(x = 1) = μ`. The maximum likelihood μ is the fraction of ones,
  and it depends on the data only through the count of ones: a **sufficient statistic**.
- **Binomial** (3.1.2): the number of ones in N independent trials.
- **Multinomial** (3.1.3): one of K outcomes, written as a **one-hot** vector, like (0, 0, 1, 0). This is how class
  labels are encoded in every classifier you'll build. Watch for the softmax appearing when the book rewrites it.

## The multivariate Gaussian (3.2)

The single most important distribution in the book. In D dimensions it has a mean vector μ and a D × D covariance
matrix Σ.

**Geometry (3.2.1).** The key quantity is the **Mahalanobis distance** `Δ² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)`. Points with the same
Δ lie on an ellipse (an ellipsoid in more dimensions). The ellipse's axes point along the **eigenvectors** of Σ, and
their lengths are the square roots of the **eigenvalues**. In the rotated coordinates of the eigenvectors, the Gaussian
splits into D independent one-dimensional Gaussians. If your eigenvectors are rusty, revisit *Maths for ML 2*.

**Moments and limitations (3.2.2–3.2.3).** A full covariance has about D²/2 numbers, which is a lot when D is large
(an image has thousands of dimensions). Diagonal or isotropic covariances are cheaper but less flexible. And a single
Gaussian has only one peak. Both problems motivate the mixtures and latent variables of later chapters.

**Conditionals and marginals (3.2.4–3.2.5).** Split x into two parts, xa and xb. If x is Gaussian, then:

- the **conditional** `p(xa | xb)` is Gaussian, and its mean is a **linear function** of xb;
- the **marginal** `p(xa)` is Gaussian too, and you get it by simply reading off the matching parts of μ and Σ.

Learn the technique the book uses, **completing the square**: if the log of a density is quadratic in a variable, the
density is Gaussian, and you can read its mean and covariance from the quadratic. You'll see this trick many times.

**Bayes for Gaussians (3.2.6).** If x is Gaussian and y depends linearly on x with Gaussian noise, then p(y) and p(x | y)
are both Gaussian, with formulas you can look up. This is the engine behind Bayesian linear regression and, later,
diffusion models.

**Maximum likelihood and sequential estimation (3.2.7–3.2.8).** The maximum likelihood mean and covariance are the
sample mean and sample covariance. The sequential version updates the mean one point at a time: new estimate = old
estimate + (1/N) × (new point − old estimate). That "move a little toward the error" shape is a first glimpse of
gradient-style learning.

**Mixtures of Gaussians (3.2.9).** A weighted sum of K Gaussians can model data with several clusters. Fitting one needs
the EM algorithm, which waits for part 4.
