# Capacitors and timing

## What a capacitor does

A **capacitor** stores a small amount of charge, like a tiny, very fast rechargeable tank. Its size is its **capacitance**, in **farads** (F); practical values are tiny: µF (microfarads), nF and pF.

Three behaviours to remember:

1. It **charges up** through a resistor over time, and **discharges** the same way.
2. It **smooths** voltage: it absorbs bumps and fills dips, like a water tower.
3. It **blocks steady DC** but lets changing signals through.

| Type | Values | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic | 10 pF to 10 µF | no polarity; used for decoupling and filters |
| Electrolytic | 1 µF to 10,000 µF | **polarized**: the − leg is marked with a stripe; backwards it can burst |
| Film | 1 nF to 10 µF | stable; audio and timing |

Every capacitor also has a **voltage rating**. Use one rated well above the circuit voltage (for example 16 V or 25 V parts in a 5 V circuit).

## Charging through a resistor: the time constant

![A 5 V battery charging a 100 µF capacitor through 10 kΩ](el-rc.png)

**How to read it:** when you close the switch S, current flows from the battery through the 10 kΩ resistor into the capacitor (the two parallel plates on the right). The resistor slows the flow, so the capacitor fills up gradually instead of instantly.

The **time constant** τ (tau) = R × C tells you how fast:

- after 1 τ the capacitor reaches about 63% of the supply
- after 5 τ it's practically full (over 99%)

```python
import math

R = 10_000         # ohms
C = 100e-6         # farads (100 µF)
tau = R * C
print(f"tau = {tau:.1f} s")
for n in [1, 2, 3, 5]:
    v = 5 * (1 - math.exp(-n))
    print(f"after {n * tau:.0f} s: {v:.2f} V ({v / 5:.0%})")
```

```output
tau = 1.0 s
after 1 s: 3.16 V (63%)
after 2 s: 4.32 V (86%)
after 3 s: 4.75 V (95%)
after 5 s: 4.97 V (99%)
```

This curve is how simple circuits measure time: a "delay" or a blinking light is often just an RC charging until it crosses some threshold. The 555 timer in module 5 does exactly that.

## Smoothing and decoupling

When a chip switches, it suddenly draws current. The long wires from the power supply can't deliver it instantly, so the voltage at the chip dips for a moment, which can make it misbehave or reset. A **decoupling capacitor** (usually **100 nF** ceramic) placed right next to the chip's power pins supplies those quick bursts.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  P[power supply] -- long wires --> C1["100 µF<br/>bulk capacitor"]
  C1 --> C2["100 nF next to<br/>each chip's VCC pin"]
  C2 --> IC[chip]
```

Rule you'll use on every board: **one 100 nF capacitor per chip power pin, as close to the pin as possible**, plus a larger capacitor (10 µF to 100 µF) where power enters the board.

## Energy stored

**E = ½ × C × V²**. Large capacitors in power supplies can hold a dangerous amount of energy and stay charged after the power is off. The small ones in this course are harmless, but it's a good habit to discharge capacitors through a resistor before handling a board.

```python
for c, v in [(100e-6, 5), (1000e-6, 12), (0.1, 5)]:
    print(f"{c * 1e6:.0f} µF at {v} V stores {0.5 * c * v ** 2 * 1000:.1f} mJ")
```

```output
100 µF at 5 V stores 1.2 mJ
1000 µF at 12 V stores 72.0 mJ
100000 µF at 5 V stores 1250.0 mJ
```

## Try it

Build the RC circuit with 10 kΩ and 100 µF (stripe to ground). Measure the capacitor's voltage while you close the switch, and count seconds: about 3.2 V after 1 s, nearly 5 V after 5 s. Then replace the battery with a wire to discharge it through the resistor and watch it fall the same way.

## Summary

- Capacitors store charge (F, µF, nF, pF); electrolytics are polarized; respect the voltage rating.
- RC time constant τ = R × C: 63% after 1 τ, practically full after 5 τ.
- Put 100 nF next to every chip's power pin, plus a bigger capacitor where power enters.
