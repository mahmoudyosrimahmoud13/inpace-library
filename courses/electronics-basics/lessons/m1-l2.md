# Ohm's law and power

## Ohm's law

The three quantities are tied together by one simple rule:

**V = I × R**   (volts = amperes × ohms)

Rearranged:

- **I = V / R**: current from voltage and resistance
- **R = V / I**: the resistance needed for a current

The circuit from the last lesson: 9 V across 450 Ω.

```python
V = 9          # volts
R = 450        # ohms
I = V / R      # amperes
print(f"I = {I:.3f} A = {I * 1000:.0f} mA")
```

```output
I = 0.020 A = 20 mA
```

A memory trick: cover the one you want in this triangle.

```text
      V
    -----
    I | R
```

## Working with units

Electronics uses prefixes all the time:

| Prefix | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| k (kilo) | × 1000 | 4.7 kΩ = 4700 Ω |
| M (mega) | × 1,000,000 | 1 MΩ |
| m (milli) | ÷ 1000 | 20 mA = 0.020 A |
| µ (micro) | ÷ 1,000,000 | 100 µF |
| n (nano) | ÷ 1,000,000,000 | 100 nF |

Convert to base units (V, A, Ω) before calculating, and most mistakes disappear. A handy shortcut: **volts ÷ kΩ = mA**. 5 V across 1 kΩ is 5 mA.

```python
for volts, kohm in [(5, 1), (5, 10), (12, 4.7)]:
    print(f"{volts} V across {kohm} kΩ -> {volts / kohm:.2f} mA")
```

```output
5 V across 1 kΩ -> 5.00 mA
5 V across 10 kΩ -> 0.50 mA
12 V across 4.7 kΩ -> 2.55 mA
```

## Power: when things get hot

**Power** is how fast energy is used, in **watts** (W):

**P = V × I**, and with Ohm's law also **P = I² × R** = **V² / R**

A resistor turns that power into heat. Ordinary resistors are rated ¼ W (0.25 W). Go above it and they get hot, change value, or burn.

```python
def resistor_power(volts, ohms):
    current = volts / ohms
    return volts * current

for ohms in [1000, 100, 47]:
    p = resistor_power(5, ohms)
    verdict = "fine for a 1/4 W resistor" if p <= 0.25 else "TOO MUCH for 1/4 W"
    print(f"5 V across {ohms} Ω: {p:.3f} W, {verdict}")
```

```output
5 V across 1000 Ω: 0.025 W, fine for a 1/4 W resistor
5 V across 100 Ω: 0.250 W, fine for a 1/4 W resistor
5 V across 47 Ω: 0.532 W, TOO MUCH for 1/4 W
```

(Exactly at the rating is too close in practice. Aim for half the rating or less.)

## Reading resistor colour codes

Small resistors show their value with coloured bands. For the common 4-band type: two digits, then a multiplier, then tolerance.

| Colour | Digit | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| black | 0 | ×1 |
| brown | 1 | ×10 |
| red | 2 | ×100 |
| orange | 3 | ×1k |
| yellow | 4 | ×10k |
| green | 5 | ×100k |
| blue | 6 | ×1M |
| violet | 7 | |
| grey | 8 | |
| white | 9 | |

**Yellow, violet, red, gold** = 4, 7, ×100 = 4700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ, ±5%. A gold last band means ±5%. When in doubt, measure it with the multimeter.

## Try it

On the breadboard: a 9 V battery and a 1 kΩ resistor. Predict the current (9 mA), then measure it with the multimeter in the loop. Measure the battery voltage too: a "9 V" battery is often 9.4 V new and less when nearly empty, which changes the current. Real parts never match the maths exactly, and measuring shows you by how much.

## Summary

- Ohm's law: V = I × R; convert units first; V ÷ kΩ = mA.
- Power P = V × I = I²R = V²/R; keep resistors well under their rating (usually ¼ W).
- Read resistor colour bands, then confirm with the meter.
