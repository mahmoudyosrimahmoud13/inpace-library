# LEDs, diodes and reading datasheets

## Diodes: one-way valves

A **diode** lets current flow in one direction only: from the **anode** (+) to the **cathode** (−, marked with a band). Backwards, it blocks.

When it conducts, a silicon diode "uses up" about **0.7 V** (its **forward voltage**). Uses:

- protecting a circuit from a battery connected backwards
- turning AC into DC (next module)
- the **flyback diode** that protects transistors from motors and relays (module 4)

## LEDs

An **LED** (light-emitting diode) is a diode that glows. Its forward voltage depends on the colour:

| Colour | Typical forward voltage |
|---|---|
| red, yellow | 1.8 to 2.2 V |
| green | 2.0 to 3.2 V (varies a lot by type) |
| blue, white | 2.8 to 3.4 V |

The long leg is the anode (+); the flat side of the case is the cathode (−).

## Why an LED needs a resistor

An LED has almost no resistance once it conducts. Connect it straight to 5 V and the current shoots up until the LED (or the battery) is damaged. A **current-limiting resistor** in series sets the current:

![As built: the battery's +, the 150 Ω resistor, the LED's long leg; the short leg back to −](el-led-real.png)

![The schematic: +5 V at the top, the resistor, the LED, and ground at the bottom](el-led.png)

**How to read these pictures:** current flows from **+5 V** (red, top of the schematic) down through the resistor, then into the LED's **long leg** (+), out of its **short leg** (−), and back to ground (blue). In schematics the LED's triangle points the way current flows; the line at the tip is the − side. The ground symbol at the bottom simply means "connected back to the battery's −".

**R = (Vsupply − V_LED) / I_LED**

```python
def led_resistor(v_supply, v_led, i_ma):
    r = (v_supply - v_led) / (i_ma / 1000)
    return r

r = led_resistor(5, 2.0, 20)
print(f"exact: {r:.0f} Ω")

standard = [100, 120, 150, 180, 220, 270, 330, 390, 470, 560, 680, 820, 1000]
pick = min(x for x in standard if x >= r)            # round up: a little less current is safe
print(f"use {pick} Ω -> {(5 - 2.0) / pick * 1000:.1f} mA")
print(f"white LED on 5 V at 10 mA: {led_resistor(5, 3.1, 10):.0f} Ω")
```

```output
exact: 150 Ω
use 150 Ω -> 20.0 mA
white LED on 5 V at 10 mA: 190 Ω
```

Resistors come in standard values (the E12 series above: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82, times powers of ten). Round **up** to the next standard value. Modern LEDs are bright at 5 to 10 mA, so 220 Ω or 330 Ω on 5 V is a safe everyday choice.

## Reading a datasheet

Every part has a **datasheet**: the manufacturer's document with its limits and behaviour. Search "part number + datasheet". For an LED, look for:

| Datasheet line | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Forward voltage (V_F) | voltage it uses when on | 2.0 V typical |
| Forward current (I_F) | normal operating current | 20 mA |
| Absolute maximum I_F | never exceed | 30 mA |
| Reverse voltage (V_R) | max backwards voltage | 5 V |
| Luminous intensity | brightness | 200 mcd at 20 mA |

Two rules for any datasheet:

1. **Absolute maximum ratings** are limits for not destroying the part, not values to design for. Stay well below them.
2. Use the **typical** values for calculations and the **min/max** values to check your design still works in the worst case.

## Several LEDs

- **Each LED in parallel needs its own resistor.** LEDs never share current evenly; one would hog it and burn out.
- In **series**, forward voltages add: three red LEDs need about 6 V plus some headroom for the resistor, so they work from 9 V but not from 5 V.

```python
leds_in_series = 3
v_needed = leds_in_series * 2.0
print(f"{leds_in_series} red LEDs need {v_needed} V; from 9 V at 15 mA: {(9 - v_needed) / 0.015:.0f} Ω")
```

```output
3 red LEDs need 6.0 V; from 9 V at 15 mA: 200 Ω
```

## Try it

Build the LED circuit on 5 V with 330 Ω. Measure the voltage across the LED and across the resistor; they add up to about 5 V. Then swap in 1 kΩ: dimmer, and the current drops. Finally, turn the LED around: nothing lights, because the diode blocks.

## Summary

- Diodes conduct one way (anode to cathode) and use about 0.7 V; LEDs use 2 to 3.4 V depending on colour.
- Always put a resistor in series with an LED: R = (Vsupply − V_LED) / I; round up to a standard value.
- Datasheets give typical values to design with and absolute maximums to stay far away from.
