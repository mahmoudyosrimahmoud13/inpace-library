# Inputs: buttons, pull-ups and sensors

Outputs make things happen; inputs tell your circuit what's going on. This lesson is about getting clean, reliable input signals.

## The floating input problem

A digital input reads HIGH (near the supply voltage) or LOW (near 0 V). But a pin connected to **nothing** isn't LOW: it **floats**, picking up noise from your hand or nearby wires, and reads random values.

A button alone only connects the pin to something while pressed. When released, the pin floats. The fix is a resistor that pulls the pin to a known level when the button is open.

## Pull-up resistors

![A 10 kΩ pull-up to 5 V, the input pin in the middle, and a button to ground](el-pullup.png)

**How to read it:** the resistor connects the input pin (green) up to +5 V, so with the button open the pin sees 5 V. Pressing the button connects that same point straight down to ground, so the pin sees 0 V.

- Released: the resistor pulls the pin up to 5 V → reads HIGH.
- Pressed: the button connects the pin straight to ground → reads LOW. Only a small current flows through the resistor.

```python
v, r = 5, 10_000
print(f"current while pressed: {v / r * 1000:.1f} mA")
```

```output
current while pressed: 0.5 mA
```

Note the logic is **inverted**: pressed = LOW. That's normal. Most microcontrollers have **internal pull-ups** you can switch on in code (on Arduino: `pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP)`), so you often need just the button.

A **pull-down** is the mirror image: resistor to ground, button to the supply. Pressed = HIGH.

## Bouncing

A mechanical button doesn't close cleanly: its contacts bounce for a few milliseconds, so one press can look like ten.

```text
press  ─┐ ┌┐ ┌┐ ┌──────────────
        └─┘└─┘└─┘      (a few ms of bouncing, then steady)
```

Two fixes:

- **Software debounce**: after a change, ignore the pin for about 20 to 50 ms, or require the reading to stay the same for that long. This is what you'll usually do.
- **Hardware debounce**: a small capacitor (100 nF) across the button with the pull-up forms an RC filter that smooths the bounces (τ = 10 kΩ × 100 nF = 1 ms).

## Analog sensors with a divider

Many sensors are **resistors whose value changes**: an **LDR** (light-dependent resistor) or a **thermistor** (temperature). Put one in a voltage divider with a fixed resistor, and the middle voltage changes with light or temperature. A microcontroller's **analog input (ADC)** measures that voltage.

```python
def divider_out(vin, r_top, r_bottom):
    return vin * r_bottom / (r_top + r_bottom)

fixed = 10_000
for light, r_ldr in [("dark room", 200_000), ("indoors", 10_000), ("bright sun", 500)]:
    v = divider_out(5, r_ldr, fixed)          # LDR on top, fixed resistor to ground
    adc = round(v / 5 * 1023)                  # a 10-bit ADC reads 0 to 1023
    print(f"{light:11}: {v:.2f} V -> ADC {adc}")
```

```output
dark room  : 0.24 V -> ADC 49
indoors    : 2.50 V -> ADC 512
bright sun : 4.76 V -> ADC 974
```

Pick the fixed resistor close to the sensor's resistance in the middle of the range you care about; that gives the biggest change in voltage.

## Digital sensors

Many modern sensors (temperature, humidity, motion, distance) send numbers over a **digital bus** instead of a voltage:

| Bus | Wires | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **I²C** | 2 (SDA, SCL) + power | sensors, small displays; many devices on the same two wires |
| **SPI** | 4 + power | fast: SD cards, displays |
| **UART** (serial) | 2 (TX, RX) | GPS, Bluetooth modules, talking to a PC |

I²C lines need pull-up resistors too (often 4.7 kΩ), though most sensor modules already include them.

## Voltage levels: 5 V vs 3.3 V

A 3.3 V chip can be damaged by a 5 V signal on its inputs. When connecting the two, use a divider (for slow signals going into the 3.3 V chip) or a small **level shifter** module. Going the other way, 3.3 V is usually high enough to count as HIGH for a 5 V chip, but check its datasheet.

## Summary

- Never leave inputs floating: use a pull-up (pressed = LOW) or pull-down; internal pull-ups often exist.
- Buttons bounce: debounce in software (20 to 50 ms) or with an RC filter.
- Resistive sensors go in a divider read by an ADC; digital sensors use I²C, SPI or UART; mind 5 V vs 3.3 V.
