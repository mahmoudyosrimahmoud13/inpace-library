# Pawn structure: the skeleton of the position

Pieces move around, but pawns can't go back. The **pawn structure** decides where the pieces belong and what plans make sense.

## Pawn islands and weaknesses

- **Isolated pawn:** no pawns of the same color on the files next to it. It can't be defended by a pawn, and the square in front of it is a perfect home for an enemy piece.
- **Doubled pawns:** two pawns on the same file. Usually weak, because they can't protect each other.
- **Backward pawn:** a pawn that has fallen behind its neighbors and can't safely move forward.
- **Passed pawn:** no enemy pawns in front of it or on the files next to it. It can run to promotion. **Passed pawns must be pushed.**

## Open files and outposts

- An **open file** has no pawns on it. **Rooks belong on open files**, and a rook that reaches the 7th rank (2nd for Black) is very strong.
- An **outpost** is a square that **no enemy pawn can attack**, protected by your own pawn. A knight on an outpost deep in enemy territory can be worth more than a rook.

## Good and bad bishops

A bishop is **bad** when your own pawns are fixed on its color, blocking it. It's **good** when the pawns are on the other color. So: put your pawns on the **opposite color** of your bishop, and on the **same color** as your opponent's bishop if you want to restrict it.

## Pawn breaks

A **pawn break** is a pawn move that attacks the enemy pawn chain to open lines (for example ...c5 or ...e5 in the Queen's Gambit Declined, or d4 in the Italian). Most middlegame plans start with the question: **which pawn break do I want, and how do I prepare it?**

## Try it

Your pawns stand on d4 and e5 (dark squares) and you have a dark-squared bishop. Is it good or bad? What can you do about it?

**Answer:** it's **bad**: your own pawns block its diagonals. Trade it for an enemy piece, move it outside the pawn chain (in front of the pawns), or, long term, change the pawn structure.
