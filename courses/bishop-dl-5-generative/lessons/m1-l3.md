# Build: a GAN in one dimension, and mode collapse

Before training a GAN on images in the module project, it's worth seeing the whole game where every number is visible.
Here the "data" is a single number per example, drawn from a Gaussian with mean 3 and standard deviation 0.5. The
generator has two parameters, the discriminator three, and you'll write every gradient yourself. You'll also meet the
failure section 17.1.2 warns about: **mode collapse**.

## 1. The two players

The generator turns noise into data: `g(z) = a z + b` with `z ~ N(0, 1)`, so its samples are Gaussian with mean `b` and
standard deviation `|a|`. To win, it needs `b = 3` and `|a| = 0.5`.

The discriminator gives the probability that `x` is real: `d(x) = σ(w0 + w1 x + w2 x²)`. The `x²` term matters: with it,
the discriminator can notice when the generator's samples are too spread out or too bunched up, not only when they sit
in the wrong place.

```python
import numpy as np

sig = lambda u: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-u))
feat = lambda x: np.stack([np.ones_like(x), x, x ** 2], 1)    # the discriminator's inputs: 1, x, x²

def train(lr_d, lr_g, d_steps, steps=20000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    a, b, w = 1.0, 0.0, np.zeros(3)
    for step in range(steps):
        for _ in range(d_steps):
            # discriminator: gradient ASCENT on  mean log d(real) + mean log(1 - d(fake))   (minus equation 17.6)
            xr = rng.normal(3.0, 0.5, 64)
            xf = a * rng.normal(0, 1, 64) + b
            dr, df = sig(feat(xr) @ w), sig(feat(xf) @ w)
            w += lr_d * (feat(xr).T @ (1 - dr) / 64 - feat(xf).T @ df / 64)
        # generator: gradient ascent on  mean log d(g(z))   (the non-saturating loss, equation 17.10)
        z = rng.normal(0, 1, 64)
        xf = a * z + b
        df = sig(feat(xf) @ w)
        dlog_dx = (1 - df) * (w[1] + 2 * w[2] * xf)          # d/dx of log d(x), by the chain rule
        a += lr_g * np.mean(dlog_dx * z)                     # x = a z + b, so dx/da = z ...
        b += lr_g * np.mean(dlog_dx)                         # ... and dx/db = 1
    return a, b, w

print("target: mean 3.0, standard deviation 0.5")
```

```output
target: mean 3.0, standard deviation 0.5
```

Two lines deserve a second look:

- The discriminator's gradient for logistic regression is the familiar "(target − prediction) × input" from part 1:
  `(1 − d)` pushes real examples up, `−d` pushes fakes down.
- The generator never sees real data. It only follows the discriminator's gradient `d log d(x) / dx`, which says "move
  your samples this way to look more real". That's the whole training signal.

## 2. One step each: mode collapse

```python
a, b, w = train(lr_d=0.05, lr_g=0.02, d_steps=1)
print(f"generator: mean {b:.3f}, standard deviation {abs(a):.3f}")
```

```output
generator: mean 3.023, standard deviation 0.001
```

The mean is right, but the standard deviation has shrunk to almost **zero**: the generator outputs essentially the same
number, about 3.0, every time. That's mode collapse in its purest form. The generator found one value the discriminator
currently likes and put everything there. A discriminator that's too weak and too slow never punishes the lack of
variety hard enough, so the generator has no reason to spread out.

## 3. A stronger discriminator

The usual remedy is to keep the discriminator ahead: several discriminator steps per generator step, and a larger
discriminator learning rate.

```python
a, b, w = train(lr_d=0.2, lr_g=0.005, d_steps=5)
print(f"generator: mean {b:.3f}, standard deviation {abs(a):.3f}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
xr, xf = rng.normal(3.0, 0.5, 100_000), a * rng.normal(0, 1, 100_000) + b
print(f"average d on real samples {sig(feat(xr) @ w).mean():.3f}, on generated samples {sig(feat(xf) @ w).mean():.3f}")
```

```output
generator: mean 2.964, standard deviation 0.525
average d on real samples 0.037, on generated samples 0.036
```

Now the generator matches the data closely: mean near 3, spread near 0.5. And look at the discriminator: it gives real
and generated samples almost the same average score, so it can no longer tell them apart. Its scores aren't 0.5, because
training is still pushing it back and forth, but separating the two is what matters. Section 17.1 describes the ideal end
point: if the generator matched the data perfectly, the best a discriminator could do is answer 0.5 for everything.

## What to take away

- A GAN's generator learns from the discriminator's gradient, never from the data directly.
- There's no single loss going down: two players move against each other, and their balance decides the result.
- Mode collapse is easy to cause even in one dimension. On images it shows up as a GAN that makes only a few kinds of
  picture. You'll measure it in the module project.
