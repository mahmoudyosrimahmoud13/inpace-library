# Build: noise in, noise out, with a perfect denoiser

A diffusion model has two halves (sections 20.1 and 20.2). The **forward** process slowly destroys data with noise; it
has no parameters and you can compute anything about it exactly. The **reverse** process undoes the noise one step at a
time, and it needs a network that predicts the noise. Today you check the forward process against its closed form, then
run the reverse process with a *perfect* noise predictor, computed exactly, for data so simple that this is possible.
The module project then trains a network to do the same job on real images.

The notation is the book's: `β_t` is the noise added at step `t`, and `α_t = (1 − β_1)(1 − β_2)…(1 − β_t)`.

## 1. The forward process

Data: numbers that are either −2 or +2 (half each). Each forward step shrinks the value a little and adds a little noise:
`z_t = √(1 − β_t) z_{t−1} + √β_t ε`.

```python
import numpy as np

T = 500
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.04, T)
alpha = np.cumprod(1 - beta)                       # alpha_t, the product up to t

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.choice([-2.0, 2.0], 200_000)
z = x.copy()
for t in range(T):
    z = np.sqrt(1 - beta[t]) * z + np.sqrt(beta[t]) * rng.normal(size=z.shape)
    if t + 1 in (10, 50, 200, 500):
        # the diffusion kernel (section 20.1.1): z_t given x is Gaussian, mean sqrt(alpha_t) x, variance 1 - alpha_t
        right = x > 0
        print(f"step {t + 1:3d}: mean of z for x = +2 is {z[right].mean():+.3f} (formula {np.sqrt(alpha[t]) * 2:+.3f}), "
              f"variance {z[right].var():.3f} (formula {1 - alpha[t]:.3f})")
print(f"at the end, z has mean {z.mean():+.3f} and variance {z.var():.3f}: practically N(0, 1)")
```

```output
step  10: mean of z for x = +2 is +1.996 (formula +1.995), variance 0.005 (formula 0.005)
step  50: mean of z for x = +2 is +1.898 (formula +1.900), variance 0.098 (formula 0.098)
step 200: mean of z for x = +2 is +0.888 (formula +0.890), variance 0.805 (formula 0.802)
step 500: mean of z for x = +2 is +0.014 (formula +0.012), variance 0.998 (formula 1.000)
at the end, z has mean +0.002 and variance 0.998: practically N(0, 1)
```

Simulating 500 small steps gives exactly what the closed form predicts. That closed form is what makes training cheap:
to make a training example at step `t`, you don't run `t` steps, you jump straight there with one formula (it's the
first lines of Algorithm 20.1). And after `T` steps, the data is forgotten: whatever you started from, you end at a
standard Gaussian.

## 2. The perfect noise predictor

The network in a diffusion model is trained to look at a noisy `z_t` and predict the noise `ε` in it (section 20.2.4).
For our two-point data the best possible prediction can be worked out by hand. Given `z_t`, Bayes' theorem says how
likely it is that the clean value was +2 or −2 (each option gives `z_t` a Gaussian likelihood), so the expected clean
value is

```text
E[x | z_t] = 2 · p(x = +2 | z_t) − 2 · p(x = −2 | z_t)
```

and since `z_t = √α_t x + √(1 − α_t) ε`, the best noise prediction is `ε* = (z_t − √α_t E[x | z_t]) / √(1 − α_t)`.

```python
def eps_star(z, t):
    a = alpha[t]
    log_lik = lambda c: -(z - np.sqrt(a) * c) ** 2 / (2 * (1 - a))      # log N(z | sqrt(a) c, 1 - a), up to a constant
    p_plus = 1 / (1 + np.exp(log_lik(-2.0) - log_lik(2.0)))             # Bayes' theorem, equal priors
    x_hat = 2 * p_plus - 2 * (1 - p_plus)
    return (z - np.sqrt(a) * x_hat) / np.sqrt(1 - a)

z = np.array([3.0, 0.1, -1.0])
for t in (9, 249, 499):
    print(f"step {t + 1:3d}: predicted noise for z = 3.0, 0.1, -1.0 ->", np.round(eps_star(z, t), 3))
```

```output
step  10: predicted noise for z = 3.0, 0.1, -1.0 -> [ 14.83  -27.98   14.694]
step 250: predicted noise for z = 3.0, 0.1, -1.0 -> [ 2.568  0.068 -0.721]
step 500: predicted noise for z = 3.0, 0.1, -1.0 -> [ 3.   0.1 -1. ]
```

Early on (step 10), very little noise has been added (its standard deviation is only about 0.07), so any `z` far from
±2 must contain a huge amount of noise relative to that: `z = 0.1` sits about 1.9 away from +2 and −2, which is more
than 25 noise standard deviations, hence the prediction of about −28. Halfway through, the predictions are moderate. At the
last step `z` is almost pure noise, and the best guess for the noise is `z` itself.

## 3. The reverse process

Now Algorithm 20.2, exactly as written: start from pure noise, and at every step remove a fraction of the predicted
noise and add a little fresh noise, except at the last step.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
z = rng.normal(size=100_000)                        # z_T: pure noise
for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
    mean = (z - beta[t] / np.sqrt(1 - alpha[t]) * eps_star(z, t)) / np.sqrt(1 - beta[t])
    z = mean + np.sqrt(beta[t]) * rng.normal(size=z.shape) if t > 0 else mean
print(f"share of samples near +2: {np.mean(np.abs(z - 2) < 0.2):.3f}")
print(f"share of samples near -2: {np.mean(np.abs(z + 2) < 0.2):.3f}")
print(f"anything else:            {np.mean((np.abs(z - 2) >= 0.2) & (np.abs(z + 2) >= 0.2)):.3f}")
```

```output
share of samples near +2: 0.501
share of samples near -2: 0.499
anything else:            0.000
```

From pure noise, 500 small denoising steps rebuild the data distribution: half the samples at +2, half at −2, and almost
nothing in between. Each sample "decides" gradually which value to become as the noise falls away.

For real images nobody can write `E[x | z_t]` down: it would mean averaging over every image that could have produced
this noisy picture. That's what the network learns from examples, with the simple loss of Algorithm 20.1: predict the
noise, squared error. The sampler stays exactly the same.

## What to take away

- The forward process has a closed form, so training examples at any noise level cost one line.
- The reverse process only needs a noise predictor; with a perfect one, it rebuilds the data distribution exactly.
- Training a diffusion model means learning that noise predictor from examples.
