# Your report, and where to go next

## Write one strong report

Recruiters and interviewers read **one** project carefully, not twenty. Pick your favourite project from the whole series
and give its README a one-page report:

1. **The question**, in one sentence a non-specialist would understand.
2. **The data**: where it comes from, how big it is, its licence, and anything odd about it.
3. **The method**: the model, the loss, the training, in a few bullet points.
4. **Results**: a table with a simple baseline in the first row, and one or two pictures.
5. **What didn't work**: the honest part. Several of your projects had one (the transformer that lost to a bag of words,
   the regularizers that changed nothing, the GAN that collapsed). These are the most convincing paragraphs you can write.
6. **What you'd do next** with more time or a GPU.

Then pin your best three repositories on your GitHub profile, and put the strongest one, with its numbers, on your CV.

## Where to go next

- **Scale up.** Rerun one project on a GPU (Google Colab is free): the Shakespeare GPT with 6 layers, the CIFAR-10 CNN for
  30 epochs, the diffusion model with more steps. Report how the numbers change.
- **Pre-trained models.** Most real work starts from a pre-trained network. The library's *Hugging Face and the deep
  learning ecosystem* and *LLMs* courses show how to fine-tune one.
- **Go deeper in one direction.** Diffusion and flow matching for generation, graph networks for science, or
  reinforcement learning. Each has a canonical paper the book cites at the end of its chapter: read one, and reproduce a
  small result from it. That's the best project of all.
- **Teach it.** Explain one of your projects in a short blog post or video. If you can explain it simply, you understand
  it.

## Optional videos

  a GPT trained on Shakespeare, like yours, at a larger scale. (The library also has a full course built on this series.)
