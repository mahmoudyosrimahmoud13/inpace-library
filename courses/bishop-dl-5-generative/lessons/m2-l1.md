# Read: normalizing flows (chapter 18)

Read chapter 18 (pages 547 to 562).

## The idea (opening)

A GAN's generator gives samples but no likelihood. A **normalizing flow** keeps both, by requiring the network to be
**invertible**: `x = f(z, w)` and `z = f⁻¹(x, w)`. Then the change-of-variables formula (equation 18.1) gives the exact
density of any data point: the simple density of `z`, times the absolute determinant of the Jacobian of `f⁻¹` (18.2).
So flows can be trained by plain maximum likelihood (18.3).

Two practical requirements follow, and the whole chapter is about meeting them:

1. The inverse must be easy to compute.
2. The Jacobian determinant must be cheap: a general `D × D` determinant costs about `D³`, impossible for images.

Stacking layers helps (18.5 to 18.7): the inverse of a composition is the composition of inverses, and log-determinants
add up layer by layer. Note too that `z` must have the same dimension as `x`: no squeezing into a small code here.

## Coupling flows (18.1)

**Real NVP**: split the vector in two; one half passes through unchanged and computes a scale and a shift for the other
half (18.10, 18.11). It's invertible whatever networks compute the scale and shift, and its Jacobian is triangular
(18.14), so the log-determinant is just the sum of the log-scales. Alternate which half stays fixed from layer to layer. Figure 18.3 shows real NVP learning the "two moons" data set, one of the three shapes in your project.
You'll build one by hand in the Build lesson and train a full one in the project.

## Autoregressive flows (18.2)

Make each output depend only on the variables before it. A **masked autoregressive flow** (MAF) computes the likelihood
in one parallel pass but samples slowly, one variable at a time; the **inverse autoregressive flow** (IAF) is the reverse:
fast sampling, slow likelihood (figure 18.4). Which to choose depends on which operation you need to be fast. Coupling
flows are a special case that is fast both ways, at some cost in flexibility.

## Continuous flows (18.3)

Read this section for the big picture.

- **Neural ODEs** (18.3.1): a residual network with infinitely many, infinitely thin layers becomes a differential
  equation whose right-hand side is a network. Solve it with any ODE solver.
- **Backpropagation** through it (18.3.2) uses the **adjoint method**: no need to store every intermediate value.
- **Neural ODE flows** (18.3.3): the density changes according to the **trace** of the Jacobian rather than its
  determinant, which is much cheaper (and Hutchinson's estimator makes it cheaper still). **Flow matching** trains these
  models without simulating the ODE at all, and brings flows close to the diffusion models of chapter 20.

## What to take from the chapter

- Flows give exact likelihoods and exact samples, at the cost of invertibility constraints on the network.
- Everything is designed around cheap determinants: triangular Jacobians, autoregressive structure, or traces.
- Coupling layers are the workhorse: easy to write, and you'll write one today.
