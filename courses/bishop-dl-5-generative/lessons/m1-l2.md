# Read: generative adversarial networks (chapter 17)

Read chapter 17 (pages 533 to 546).

## The idea (opening and 17.1)

A generator `g(z, w)` turns a random vector `z ~ N(0, I)` (equation 17.1) into a data point. Together, `p(z)` and the
generator define a distribution over images, but there's no formula for its likelihood, so it can't be trained by
maximum likelihood. The GAN trick: train a second network, the **discriminator** `d(x, φ)`, to tell real examples from
generated ones, and train the generator to fool it. The density-modelling problem becomes a supervised classification
problem, with labels you make up yourself (real = 1, fake = 0).

## The loss (17.1.1)

The discriminator minimizes the ordinary cross-entropy (equations 17.5 and 17.6). The generator **maximizes** the same
error: it's a zero-sum game, so the updates for `φ` and `w` have opposite signs (17.7, 17.8). In practice they take turns,
one mini-batch step each. If the generator became perfect, the best the discriminator could do is answer 0.5 for
everything. Once trained, the discriminator is thrown away.

Note the **conditional GAN**: give both networks a label too, and you can ask for a particular class.

## Training in practice (17.1.2)

This is the part to read most carefully, because it's where GANs are hard.

- **No progress metric.** The objective goes up and down, so the loss doesn't tell you whether the pictures are improving.
- **Mode collapse.** The generator can map every `z` to a few outputs (say, only the digit 3) that the discriminator
  can't reject. You'll cause it on purpose in the Build lesson.
- **Vanishing gradients.** Figure 17.2: when real and fake are easy to separate, the optimal discriminator is flat around
  both, so the generator gets almost no gradient. Smoother discriminators help (least-squares GANs, instance noise).
- **The non-saturating loss** (17.10, figure 17.3): instead of minimizing `ln(1 − d(g(z)))`, the generator maximizes
  `ln d(g(z))`. Same goal, but strong gradients exactly when the generator is losing. Every practical GAN uses it,
  including yours.

## Image GANs (17.2)

Generators for images use **transpose convolutions** to grow an image from a small code (figure 17.4), and the
discriminator is an ordinary CNN. Growing both networks progressively, from low to high resolution, gives the striking
face images of figure 1.3. Read **CycleGAN** (17.2.1) for the idea, not the details: two generators translate between
domains (photos and paintings) without paired examples, kept honest by a **cycle-consistency** loss (going there and back
should give the original).

## What to take from the chapter

- A GAN learns a sampler without ever writing down a likelihood.
- Training is a balance between two players, not a single loss going down, which is why it's fragile.
- GANs make sharp images fast (one network pass per sample), but measuring them needs care. Since GAN loss doesn't
  measure quality, your project uses a separate judge.
