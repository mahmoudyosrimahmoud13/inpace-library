# Big project: the generators side by side

Over five days you've built a GAN, a normalizing flow, a VAE and a diffusion model, each judged in its own way: a GAN
loss that measures nothing useful, a flow's exact likelihood, a VAE's ELBO, a diffusion model's noise loss. Those numbers
can't be compared with each other. Today's big project puts three of the image generators on the **same** data, MNIST
digits, and measures them with the **same** yardsticks.

## The yardsticks

| Question | How the project measures it |
|---|---|
| Do the samples look like digits? | the share of samples a separate, well-trained digit classifier (the **judge**) is at least 90% sure about |
| Does it make every digit, or only a few? | the **effective number of digits** among the judge's answers: 10 means perfectly even, 1 means one digit only (mode collapse) |
| Does it invent, or copy? | the distance from each sample to its nearest training digit, compared with real test digits |
| How fast is it? | milliseconds per generated image on the same computer |

The flow is left out on purpose: the coupling flow you built works on 2-D points. Flows for images exist (the book cites
them), but they need much larger networks than the others to be competitive, which is itself a finding worth one line in
your report.

## What you reuse

You don't train everything again. The VAE (16-D latent) comes from day 3 and the diffusion model and the judge from day 4,
with exactly the same code; only the GAN is retrained, on digits this time. The next lesson's notebook lists the commands
for all of them, so it also works on its own.

## What to hand in

A repository with the comparison, a README with the results table and the side-by-side picture, and one paragraph that
**recommends** a model for a real situation, such as a phone app that must generate an image in under a second, or a
print where quality matters most. Recommendations backed by your own measurements are exactly what a technical
interview wants to hear.
